# Brand and growth methods

Use this module for growth sources, brand equity and distinctive assets, positioning, segmentation and targeting, brand architecture, portfolio and price-pack choices, retention economics and the brand/activation balance. Choice logic is in [strategic-choice.md](strategic-choice.md).

No brand doctrine wins universally. Select the lens by the question, the mechanism, category conditions, evidence and the method's limits. Compatible mechanisms can coexist: recognition, retrieval, physical access, perceived value, pricing power and switching can all matter at once. Do not route "FMCG to one school, premium/B2B to another" by default; test conditions. Methods are summarised in our own words; verify detailed procedures and figures against original sources before quoting them.

## 1. Question-to-lens selector

| Question | Useful lenses | Evidence that answers it |
|---|---|---|
| Where will volume growth come from? | Penetration, frequency, units per occasion, new occasions, distribution, price, mix | Buyer panel or transaction data; distribution; price and mix analysis |
| Why don't more category buyers choose us? | Mental availability, physical availability, perceived value, switching barriers | Brand retrieval in buying situations, distribution and stock data, price position, qualitative switching evidence |
| Are we easy to recognise and find? | Distinctive assets, pack and shelf visibility, availability | Asset recognition and attribution tests; store checks |
| Can we charge more? | Meaningful difference, perceived value, willingness to pay, competitive alternatives | Price response in market, price tests, choice experiments, premium tier performance |
| Which competitive territory? | Positioning, customer value, capabilities, trade-offs | Customer needs and alternatives, competitor positions, capability audit |
| Why is category behaviour changing? | Jobs/progress, occasions, economics, culture, technology | Behavioural data, qualitative research, macro and category data |
| How should the portfolio grow? | Brand roles, price tiers, cannibalisation, incrementality, permission | SKU performance and source of volume, price ladders, brand associations |
| How defensible is our position? | Distribution reach, switching costs, brand memory, scale, assets | Competitive benchmarking, cost data, customer switching behaviour |
| Subscription or repeat-service growth? | Acquisition, activation, retention, expansion, unit economics | Cohort data, churn reasons, acquisition cost, lifetime value ranges |
| How to allocate between long-term brand building and short-term response? | Effectiveness evidence, category and purchase-cycle conditions | Test-and-control history, econometric evidence where available, category norms |

## 2. Sources of growth

- **Use when**: setting growth ambitions, diagnosing where growth stalled, or checking whether a plan's growth is plausible.
- **Not when**: data cannot distinguish sources; then state the gap and the data needed.
- **Inputs**: revenue and volume by item, channel, region; buyer data where available; distribution and price.
- **Steps**: decompose revenue ([analytics.md](analytics.md): `pvm`, `contribution`); decompose volume into buyers × frequency × units where buyer data exist (`buyers`); separate distribution-driven from rate-of-sale-driven change; identify which lever is constrained and which has headroom; check margin consequences.
- **Output**: a growth map by lever with the binding constraint.
- **Example**: volume +5% = penetration +10%, frequency −5%: the brand is recruiting but buyers buy less often — examine pack, price per occasion or occasion fit.
- **Common failure**: assuming growth must come from loyalty or from penetration by doctrine.
- **Evidence limit**: decomposition locates growth; it does not tell you which lever is cheapest to move.

Empirical research in many repeat-purchase categories reports that larger brands mainly differ from smaller ones in how many buyers they have, with loyalty measures varying less. Use that as a strong prior for mass repeat-purchase categories, then test it against the client's data and conditions. Where retention is the binding constraint (subscriptions, services, contracts), retention economics can be decisive even though acquisition still matters.

## 3. Mental and physical availability

- **Use when**: the brand is under-bought relative to its quality and price; growth depends on reaching light and non-buyers.
- **Not when**: the product fails on quality or value; availability will amplify disappointment.
- **Inputs**: category entry points (the situations, needs and motivations in which people think of the category), brand retrieval in those situations, distribution and visibility data.
- **Steps**: identify the most important buying situations with evidence (not invented); measure whether the brand comes to mind in them and relative to competitors; measure physical availability (numeric and weighted distribution, stock-outs, visibility, chilled availability, pack presence in relevant channels); identify gaps where mental or physical availability limits purchase; design communications and distribution actions for the gaps.
- **Output**: a prioritised list of situations and outlets where availability limits growth.
- **Example**: high awareness but low retrieval for "evening family meal" while competitors own it; physical availability good.
- **Common failure**: treating awareness as mental availability; awareness does not show whether the brand comes to mind when buying.
- **Evidence limit**: survey retrieval measures are sensitive to question design; validate against behaviour.

## 4. Distinctive assets

- **Use when**: the brand needs to be recognised quickly and correctly attributed (packs, ads, shelves, digital thumbnails).
- **Steps**: list candidate assets (colour, logo, shape, character, sound, tagline, pack format); test recognition among category buyers and correct attribution (not confused with competitors); classify as strong, invest, test or avoid; use strong assets consistently across touchpoints; avoid changes that destroy recognition without evidence of benefit.
- **Output**: an asset strategy with keep, build and drop decisions.
- **Common failure**: a redesign driven by internal fatigue; claiming ownership of generic category cues.
- **Evidence limit**: recognition tests measure memory links, not persuasion or sales effect.

## 5. Meaningful difference, perceived value and pricing power

- **Use when**: premium pricing, trade-up, margin protection or resisting discounting is the question.
- **Not when**: the brand's problem is being noticed or found; do not jump to differentiation messaging.
- **Inputs**: price position, price response evidence, reasons for choice, competitive alternatives, cost-to-serve.
- **Steps**: identify the differences customers value enough to pay for, with behavioural evidence; test whether the brand is actually perceived as different on them; test price response (historical variation, price tests, choice experiments); compare with alternatives customers actually use; check that the difference is deliverable and sustainable.
- **Output**: a pricing-power judgement with evidence strength.
- **Common failure**: "our quality is superior" as a pricing strategy without evidence that buyers perceive it or will pay.
- **Evidence limit**: stated willingness to pay overstates real behaviour; in-market evidence is stronger.

Distinctiveness (being recognisable) and differentiation (being meaningfully different) answer different questions. Do not force a choice between them; test which one is constraining.

## 6. Positioning

- **Use when**: defining or repairing how a brand or offer should be understood relative to alternatives.
- **Inputs**: competitive alternatives as customers see them, target customers and contexts, value delivered, proof, brand assets.
- **Steps**:
  1. Identify the real alternatives (including doing nothing, informal substitutes, other categories).
  2. Choose the frame of reference (the category or use context that makes the value obvious).
  3. Define the target and context where the offer wins (people × situation, not demographics alone).
  4. State the value in terms of the customer's progress, with the differentiating capability behind it.
  5. Provide proof (product facts, performance, credible third-party evidence).
  6. Connect to distinctive assets and tone.
  7. Apply the competitor-substitution test ([strategic-choice.md](strategic-choice.md)).
  8. State what the positioning gives up.
- **Output**: a positioning statement plus proof points and trade-offs.
- **Common failure**: aspirational statements with no proof; positioning for everyone.
- **Evidence limit**: a statement's quality is not market acceptance; test comprehension and preference with target customers.

## 7. Segmentation and targeting

- **Use when**: customer heterogeneity changes the decision (different needs, economics, reachability or responses).
- **Not when**: a mass strategy with broad reach serves the objective and segments would not be treated differently.
- **Criteria for a useful segment**: meaningful (differs in behaviour, needs or economics that matter to the choice), stable (reappears across samples and time), reachable (can be found through media, channels or places), sizeable and commercially valuable, actionable (the organisation can serve it differently).
- **Steps**: start from the decision; choose segmentation variables that reflect behaviour, needs, occasions or economics; use demographics as descriptors, not explanations; test stability and reachability; size segments with representative data; select a priority investment audience.
- **Targeting**: a priority investment audience is where effort is concentrated; it does not mean permanently excluding other category buyers, who may still buy.
- **Output**: segments with size, value, behaviour, reachability, and what we do differently for each.
- **Example**: "Gen Z" is not a segment by itself; "young urban adults who buy single-serve drinks near transport hubs at least weekly" is testable if data show it differs in pack, price and outlet behaviour.
- **Common failure**: demographic personas with invented psychology; unstable clusters named as durable audiences.
- **Evidence limit**: survey-based clusters depend on variables and methods chosen; validate with behaviour.

## 8. Brand architecture and portfolio

- **Use when**: deciding brand roles, extensions, sub-brands, SKU rationalisation or launches.
- **Steps**: map brands/SKUs by role (traffic builder, profit generator, entry point, trade-up, flanker, innovation); check source of volume for launches (incremental vs cannibalised); assess brand permission for extensions with evidence; check price ladder coherence; evaluate complexity costs (distribution slots, production, working capital).
- **Output**: portfolio roles, keep/grow/fix/exit decisions, launch conditions.
- **Common failure**: counting new SKU revenue as growth without subtracting cannibalisation.
- **Evidence limit**: source-of-volume estimates from launch data need a counterfactual.

## 9. Price-pack architecture

- **Use when**: affordability, occasion fit, trade margins or inflation pressure make pack sizes and price points a lever.
- **Inputs**: price points, pack sizes, price per unit volume, channel margins, occasion data, competitor price-pack ladder, cost structure.
- **Steps**: map the price ladder by channel (including informal outlets where relevant); check price per unit and gaps between packs; identify occasions and outlets each pack serves; test affordability constraints with evidence (not assumptions about national averages); model margin and cannibalisation effects; consider trade margin and coin/note denomination practicality where cash matters; pilot before national changes.
- **Output**: price-pack options with margin, affordability and cannibalisation logic.
- **Common failure**: inferring the right pack response from headline inflation alone; inflation does not by itself establish the brand's cost exposure or buyers' response.
- **Evidence limit**: shopper response to new price points is uncertain until tested.

## 10. Retention and customer economics

- **Use when**: subscriptions, contracts, services, apps, financial products or any business with measurable repeat relationships.
- **Steps**: build cohorts by acquisition period; measure activation, retention/churn and revenue per customer over time; identify churn timing and reasons (behavioural and qualitative); estimate unit economics as ranges (acquisition cost, margin, expected lifetime); compare the value of retention improvements with acquisition improvements; design interventions at the moments of churn.
- **Output**: the binding constraint in customer economics and the value of moving it. For a board decision (e.g. "double acquisition spend?"), show a small scenario table in money terms (current plan, scaled acquisition with weaker marginal customers, retention fix at flat spend), each with contribution and capital required, then phased gates with a stop-loss rule tied to cohort results.
- **Common failure**: enforcing "penetration first" when early churn destroys acquisition spend; or ignoring acquisition when retention is already good.
- **Evidence limit**: lifetime value projections are estimates; show sensitivity to retention assumptions.

## 11. Brand building and activation over time

- **Use when**: allocating effort between long-term demand creation and short-term response, or judging campaign measurement.
- **Principles**: effects of brand-building communications typically accrue over longer periods and are hard to see in short-term response metrics; activation effects are faster and easier to measure; optimising only for short-term measurable response can erode long-term growth. Effectiveness databanks report typical allocations, but these vary by category, brand size, purchase cycle, B2B/B2C and channel economics.
- **Steps**: state the objective and horizon; identify what each activity is expected to do (build memory structures and associations, or trigger a response); plan measures for both; treat any budget ratio as contextual evidence, not a rule; use tests or econometrics where possible to learn the client's own response.
- **Common failure**: presenting a published ratio as a law; judging brand building by next-week sales.
- **Evidence limit**: databank findings are associations in submitted cases, not guaranteed outcomes.

## 12. Premiumisation and purpose claims

Before recommending premiumisation, brand purpose or a major repositioning, require:
- Business evidence: margin logic, source of volume, cannibalisation.
- Behavioural evidence: target buyers already trade up in this or adjacent categories; price response evidence.
- Credibility evidence: the brand has permission and proof; the product or business behaviour supports the claim.

If these are missing, the recommendation is conditional or research-first, with a discriminating test (for example a priced limited-outlet pilot with matched controls).
