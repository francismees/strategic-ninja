## 1. What counts as an insight

An insight is an **evidence-grounded explanation that changes understanding or a decision**. It explains why something happens, not just what happens.

It does not have to be:
- a hidden human tension (tension is one useful lens, not a requirement);
- unprecedented or surprising to everyone;
- about consumer psychology (operational, economic, category, channel and competitive explanations qualify).

A straightforward, well-evidenced observation can justify a sound action without being dressed up as a profound insight. Call it an observation and act on it.

## 2. The chain

```text
Evidence → observation → contrast/pattern → mechanism hypothesis
→ explanatory insight → strategic implication → opportunity → choice
```

| Step | Content | Test before moving on |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence | Sourced data, verbatim accounts, measurements, observed behaviour | Is it real, correctly scoped and not cherry-picked? |
| Observation | A literal description of what the evidence shows | Would a neutral reader agree it says this? |
| Contrast / pattern | A relationship across people, periods, places, products or benchmarks | Compared with what? Is the difference larger than noise? |
| Mechanism hypothesis | A proposed explanation of why the pattern exists | What else could explain it? What would disprove it? |
| Explanatory insight | The mechanism, once supported, stated in a way that changes understanding | Is it supported, specific, explanatory and decision-relevant? |
| Implication | What becomes important if it is true | Does it change a choice? |
| Opportunity | The action space it opens | Is it commercially meaningful and feasible? |
| Choice | What we will and will not do | Covered in [strategic-choice.md](strategic-choice.md) |

Never jump from data straight to insight. The two steps most often skipped are **contrast** (without a comparison there is no pattern) and **mechanism** (without an explanation there is only description).

## 3. Standards an insight must meet

1. **Supported** — the mechanism has evidence beyond the pattern it explains (qualitative accounts, behavioural data, a natural experiment, a consistent pattern across independent cuts).
2. **Specific enough to matter** — names who, when, where or which category condition. "Consumers want value" fails.
3. **Explanatory rather than descriptive** — answers why.
4. **Capable of changing a decision** — if it would not change anything, it is interesting, not strategic.
5. **Alternatives considered** — the strongest competing explanation is named, with why it is less supported.
6. **Contradictions preserved** — evidence that does not fit is recorded, not dropped.

## 4. Selective lenses

Use these as lenses where they help, not as a ritual applied to every data point:

- **Compared with what?** Establishes the contrast.
- **Why?** Moves from pattern to mechanism.
- **Why now?** Tests whether something changed (price, distribution, competitor, regulation, income, season, technology).
- **Who and when?** Locates the pattern in people and occasions.
- **What is unusual?** Anomalies often reveal the mechanism.
- **What is missing?** Absent buyers, absent occasions, absent channels.
- **What else could explain it?** Guards against the first attractive story.
- **What would disprove it?** Defines the test.

Tension or friction lenses (desire vs behaviour, promise vs experience, convention vs reality, two competing jobs) are useful when evidence shows people wanting one thing and doing another. Use them when the evidence supports them; do not manufacture a tension to make an insight sound deeper.

## 5. Kinds of insight

| Kind | Example shape (synthetic) |
|---|---|
| Consumer | Buyers who switched cite running out between shopping trips; smaller packs sold near homes reduce the gap. |
| Commercial / economic | Revenue growth came from a price increase that the largest customers absorbed and smaller outlets did not; volume loss is concentrated where price sensitivity is highest. |
| Operational | Rate of sale is similar where stocked; decline is explained by outlets that lost stock after a distributor change. |
| Channel | The brand's modern-trade growth is a listing gain, not a channel shift in the category. |
| Category | The category is growing through new occasions (on-the-go) where the brand has no suitable pack. |
| Competitive | A competitor's gain comes from outlets where it placed coolers, not from advertising. |
| Cultural | Only with local evidence of how meaning or practice shapes the category; see [../markets/tanzania.md](../markets/tanzania.md). |

## 6. Qualitative evidence and the inference boundary

- Preserve respondent or source IDs, context, authentic quotations and translation status.
- Separate what respondents **say**, what they were **observed to do**, and what the **analyst infers**.
- Never invent quotations, participants, motivations or cultural consensus.
- Themes from interviews can **suggest mechanisms**; they do not establish how common something is in a population, segment size or causality.
- Report qualitative counts as counts in the sample ("7 of 12 interviewees"), not as market percentages.
- When a transcript is ambiguous, say so and state what follow-up would resolve it.
- Synthetic personas or simulated respondents generate hypotheses, not validation.

State the boundary explicitly in outputs: **what the evidence can conclude, what it cannot conclude, and the smallest next validation step.**

## 7. Status labels

- **Defensible insight** — mechanism supported; alternatives weaker; recorded with confidence and implication.
- **Mechanism hypothesis** — plausible explanation not yet adequately supported; carry it with a test.
- **Observation** — useful fact or pattern without an established mechanism; may still justify action.
- **Rejected** — the explanation failed against evidence; keep the record so it is not reintroduced.

In records, insights use `defensible`, `mechanism_hypothesis` or `rejected`. An insight whose evidence consists only of hypotheses or assumptions cannot be `defensible`.

## 8. Failure modes

- Observation dressed as insight ("Gen Z are digital natives").
- Appealing but unsupported psychological story bridging an evidence gap.
- Insight that merely restates the brand's desired positioning.
- Universal human truths with no link to category behaviour.
- Demographic label used as explanation.
- Treating one vivid quote as a pattern.
- Rejecting a solid operational explanation because it lacks emotional resonance.
- Forcing every insight into tension language.
