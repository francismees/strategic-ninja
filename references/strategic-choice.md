## 1. What a strategy must contain

A strategy is a diagnosis of the challenge, a guiding choice about how to deal with it, and coherent actions that carry the choice out, with the resources to do so. Not a strategy:
- An objective ("grow 12%").
- An activity list ("launch TikTok, sponsor football, refresh packaging").
- A slogan or vision without choices.
- A SWOT table. SWOT may summarise an established diagnosis; it cannot replace diagnosis or choice.

## 2. Method cards

### Diagnosis → guiding policy → coherent action (Rumelt's kernel)
- **Use when**: turning a diagnosis into a strategy; testing whether a plan is a strategy at all.
- **Not when**: the diagnosis is still unknown (return to [diagnosis.md](diagnosis.md)); it will not by itself generate options.
- **Inputs**: a diagnosis naming the critical obstacle; candidate approaches.
- **Steps**: state the challenge in one or two sentences; write a guiding policy that says how to deal with it and what it rules out; list actions that reinforce each other; check each action serves the policy; remove actions that do not.
- **Output**: a short strategy statement a leader could repeat.
- **Example**: *Diagnosis*: volume decline is concentrated in wholesaler-served dukas that lost stock after a distributor switch. *Policy*: restore physical availability in the top-selling duka clusters before spending on demand creation. *Actions*: distributor service agreement with fill-rate targets; van sales to the top clusters; hold the planned media increase until availability recovers.
- **Common failure**: filling the three headings with generic content ("Diagnosis: competitive market").
- **Evidence limit**: the structure makes logic visible; it does not validate the diagnosis.

### Where to play / how to win (choice cascade)
- **Use when**: choices about segments, occasions, channels, geographies, categories and the basis of competing are interdependent.
- **Not when**: the problem is purely operational execution of an already sound strategy.
- **Inputs**: diagnosis, market and customer evidence, capabilities, economics.
- **Steps**: define the winning aspiration in measurable terms; choose where to play (who, which needs/occasions, which channels, which geographies, which categories) and where not; define how to win there (the value proposition and the advantage that delivers it); identify the capabilities the choice needs; identify the management systems (measures, processes, budgets) that sustain them; iterate — the choices must fit each other.
- **Output**: a coherent set of choices with explicit exclusions.
- **Example**: play in urban on-the-go chilled occasions through dukas and kiosks near transport hubs; win through single-serve packs at an accessible price point and cooler availability; needs cooler logistics and outlet-level distribution data; managed through weekly availability KPIs.
- **Common failure**: where-to-play answers that include everyone; how-to-win claims with no capability behind them.
- **Evidence limit**: coherence is not proof of attractiveness; test economics and customer evidence.

### Competitive position, activity fit and value chain (Porter-style lenses)
- **Use when**: judging profitability of a position, structural pressures on margin, or whether activities reinforce a distinctive position.
- **Not when**: the real issue is demand-side (buyers do not think of or find the brand) or pure execution; do not run a five-forces checklist on every question.
- **Inputs**: industry structure, cost structure, competitor positions, activity data.
- **Steps**: identify where value is created and captured along the value chain; identify pressures on price and cost (buyer power, supplier power, substitutes, entrants, rivalry) that matter for this decision; define the position (cost or differentiated value, broad or focused); map the activities that deliver it and check that they fit and are hard to copy as a system; identify trade-offs the position requires.
- **Output**: a view of why the position can earn returns and what it must give up.
- **Example**: a mass brand competing on price against informal imports needs a low-cost route-to-market; adding premium service activities would dilute the cost position.
- **Common failure**: listing forces without saying what they imply for the choice.
- **Evidence limit**: structural analysis explains pressures; it does not predict competitor behaviour precisely.

### Capabilities and defensibility
- **Use when**: testing whether an advantage is real, durable and ownable.
- **Steps**: name the claimed advantage; identify its source (scale economies, distribution reach, switching costs, network effects, brand memory structures and associations, proprietary assets or data, regulatory position, process know-how); ask whether competitors could replicate it, how quickly and at what cost; consider whether the advantage is transient and needs renewal.
- **Common failure**: calling ordinary strengths ("quality", "passion") advantages.
- **Evidence limit**: defensibility is a judgement about the future; state the assumptions.

### Jobs, progress and switching (JTBD)
- **Use when**: demand, switching, innovation or new-category questions; understanding what people are trying to achieve and what stops them changing.
- **Not when**: the question is about reach, availability or pricing mechanics already understood.
- **Inputs**: behavioural and qualitative evidence, ideally from recent switchers.
- **Steps**: identify the progress sought in a specific circumstance; map the push away from the current solution, the pull of the new one, the anxieties about switching and the habits of the present; identify which force is binding; design actions that strengthen push/pull or reduce anxiety/habit.
- **Output**: a switching explanation grounded in quotes or behaviour.
- **Common failure**: invented jobs written from the brand's point of view.
- **Evidence limit**: qualitative switching stories explain mechanisms, not prevalence.

## 3. Generating alternatives

For substantial strategy work, develop **at least two credible alternative routes plus the status quo** where relevant. Quick tasks need proportionate alternatives, not option theatre.

Alternatives must differ in meaningful choices, mechanisms, resource allocation or risk — for example:
- Fix availability first vs build demand first.
- Defend the core mass pack vs trade up to a premium tier.
- Concentrate on two regions vs national coverage.
- Retention-led vs acquisition-led growth in a subscription business.
- Partner for distribution vs build own route-to-market.

Do not create a deliberately weak alternative to flatter the preferred one. If the alternatives collapse into variations of one idea, reframe (section 4) or say that one route clearly dominates and why.

## 4. Strategic creativity

Use these when obvious options are thin or the category is stuck. Change the method if it fails to produce useful alternatives; do not rotate methods by quota.

- **Reframe the problem**: from "how do we advertise more" to "how do we get chilled product within reach at the moment of thirst".
- **Challenge category conventions**: list what every competitor does; ask which conventions exist for real constraints and which from habit.
- **Analogies**: how did another category or market solve a structurally similar problem (credit via mobile money, distribution via bus routes)? Test transfer before adopting.
- **Change the scale**: smaller packs, larger bundles, shorter time windows, one district rather than a region.
- **Combine capabilities**: what can the client do by combining assets it already has (distribution + a trusted brand + agent network)?
- **Invert**: what would make the problem worse? Reverse it.
- **Remove a constraint temporarily**: if budget were not the limit, what would work — and is there a cheaper version?

Label speculative routes clearly (`speculative: true` in records). Evaluate them separately for relevance, evidence, feasibility and economics. Imagination is for generating options; evidence and economics decide between them.

## 5. Evaluating routes

For each serious route, test:

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| Commercial mechanism | Through which lever does value arise (price, volume, margin, mix, distribution, penetration, frequency, retention, switching, cost)? |
| Customer value | Why would the target customers behave differently? What evidence says so? |
| Brand credibility | Does the brand have permission, assets and proof to deliver this? |
| Competitive response | What will competitors do? Does the route survive it? |
| Capability requirements | What must the organisation be able to do, and can it? |
| Resource demands | Money, people, time, management attention |
| Risks | What could go wrong; how bad; how likely |
| Time to impact | When would effects be visible? |
| Reversibility | Can we stop or change course cheaply? |
| Critical assumptions | What must be true? Which assumption, if false, kills the route? |

Use ranges and sensitivities when numbers are defensible; do not fabricate forecasts to fill a table. A qualitative comparison with clear reasoning beats spurious weighted scores. If numbers matter and are missing, show the break-even or threshold logic ("the pilot pays back if rate of sale rises by more than X per outlet per week").

## 6. Tests for advantage claims and copied tactics

### Competitor-substitution test (for claims of uniqueness)
Replace the brand with its nearest competitor. If the positioning or advantage claim still reads equally true, the claim is not differentiating.
- Apply it to **positioning and advantage claims**.
- Do **not** apply it as a blanket veto on sensible shared actions. Restoring distribution, fixing a price gap or improving service may be exactly right even though a competitor could do the same, if it relieves the diagnosed constraint and pays back.

### Transfer test (for a competitor's winning tactic)
Before recommending "do what the competitor did", establish:
1. **Mechanism**: why did it work for them (availability, price, novelty, a specific asset)?
2. **Evidence it worked**: performance evidence, not visibility.
3. **Brand permission**: does our brand have the credibility to do it?
4. **Capabilities**: do we have the assets, distribution and skills?
5. **Economics**: does it pay back at our scale and margins?
6. **Conditions**: do the market conditions that enabled it still hold? Will a second mover get the same effect?
Recommend only what transfers; otherwise adapt the mechanism, not the tactic.

## 7. The recommendation contract

Make the recommendation explicit:

1. **What to do** — the chosen route in one or two sentences.
2. **Why this route** — the diagnosis it addresses and the mechanism.
3. **Why now** — what makes timing matter.
4. **What not to pursue** — meaningful non-choices and rejected alternatives with reasons.
5. **What must be true** — critical assumptions.
6. **How to learn** — the measures and tests.
7. **What would reverse the choice** — concrete triggers.

State the recommendation type: **supported**, **conditional** (with the conditions) or **research/experiment first** (with the discriminating test). See the progress rules in [../SKILL.md](../SKILL.md).

## 8. Proportionality

- A quick question gets a clear point of view, the main alternative and the key assumption.
- A standard decision gets two or three routes and a qualitative evaluation.
- A deep engagement gets full option records, economics with sensitivities, and a red-team pass.
