**Short answer: don't approve a blanket doubling yet.** With 40% of customers failing in the first three months, extra acquisition spend will most likely buy more losses and burn more cash. The extra customers it brings in are unlikely to be better than today's. Offer the board a gated route to doubling instead. In the first month, find out which customers actually pay back. Test fixes for early failure with proper controls. Then release the extra spend in tranches as the evidence comes in.

## Why doubling now is the wrong order

In pay-as-you-go, you pay for the device, installation, commission and marketing up front and recover the money over the contract. A customer who stops paying in month two or three has usually paid only the deposit and a few instalments. Early failure is therefore the heart of your unit economics, not a side issue.

On your numbers (I'm assuming 8,000 is gross sales), about 4,800 customers a month are still paying at 90 days. The other 3,200 a month, roughly 38,000 a year, have used your capital and paid little of it back. Double the spend at the same rate and that becomes 6,400 a month. Three things could make it worse than a straight doubling:

- **The extra customers may be worse.** More spend reaches people your current effort misses: further away, less qualified, or signed up by agents stretching for bigger targets. Their failure rate and acquisition cost may be higher than today's average. You don't know yet, and you should before committing.
- **Cash.** Each new customer ties up working capital until they've paid. Doubling intake doubles that need while 40% of it isn't coming back. If your loans carry portfolio-quality covenants, a weaker book becomes a financing risk as well as a profit risk.
- **Rewards for connections alone.** Some income may be paid per connection, such as results-based finance, carbon revenue or investor volume targets. If that's part of the board's thinking, remember it doesn't cover bad debt and rewards sales whether or not customers pay.

## The number the board needs first: the break-even failure rate

For any segment:

> **Break-even early-failure rate = (G − U) ÷ (G − B)**
>
> - **U** = total upfront cost per customer: device, logistics, installation, commission and marketing
> - **G** = total collections from a customer who keeps paying, minus servicing costs
> - **B** = what you recover from a customer who fails early: deposit and payments made, plus the repossessed device's value, minus recovery costs

**For illustration only (these aren't your numbers):** say U = 100, G = 140 and B = 30. Each customer is then worth 40 − (110 × failure rate):

| Early-failure rate | Value per customer (upfront cost = 100) |
|---|---|
| 20% | +18 |
| 30% | +7 |
| ~36% | 0 (break-even) |
| **40% (today)** | **−4** |
| 50% | −15 |

Two points hold whatever your real inputs are:

1. **Near break-even, small changes in the failure rate swing value a lot.** In the illustration, each 10-point cut in early failure adds 11 per customer. At 8,000 sales a month, that equals the upfront cost of about 880 customers a month. Doubling spend at a 40% failure rate at best doubles the loss.
2. **The cost of the extra customers moves the threshold.** Suppose doubling raises the upfront cost of the extra customers from 100 to 115. Their break-even failure rate then drops from about 36% to about 23%. Judge the doubling on what the extra shilling buys, not on averages.

The formula leaves out two things. Financing cost pushes the break-even rate down. Customers who pause and later resume raise B, which pushes it up. That's one reason to pin down exactly what "default or deactivate" counts.

## What's likely behind the 40%

First, split the 40% into four groups:

- customers who never paid after the deposit
- customers who paid for a while, then stopped
- customers who are locked out but come back
- returns and faulty units

Each needs a different fix. Then test the possible explanations below against each other. Several can be true at once, so the job is to find which one dominates in which segment. Failure this early points more to the sale, customer selection and the first weeks than to product wear or long-run affordability. That's an inference from timing only, and your cohort data can settle it quickly.

| Explanation | What you'd see if it's true, and what would rule it out | What it points to |
|---|---|---|
| **Sales incentives.** Agents paid at installation sign up customers who can't or won't pay; some sales may be fraudulent. | Failure varies sharply by agent or distributor, jumps near sales deadlines, and many customers miss their first payment. Check-in calls find customers who don't understand the terms. *Ruled out if* agents in the same area have similar failure rates. | Change agent pay, add check-in calls, drop the worst agents |
| **Affordability and selection.** The plan is too big for the customer's cash flow, and screening is weak. | Failure concentrated among low deposits, larger systems and certain customer types, with irregular payments from the start. *Ruled out if* customers who fail look like good payers and started paying the same way. | Screening before sale; higher deposit or a smaller starter product that can be upgraded |
| **Seasonal income.** Customers pause rather than quit. | Lockouts cluster in certain months, many customers come back, and the pattern varies by region with local income cycles. *Ruled out if* few return and there's no seasonal pattern. | Change the metric; flexible or harvest-timed plans where needed |
| **Product, service or payment problems.** | Fault reports, failed payments or unlock problems come before customers stop paying, clustered by batch, model or distance from service points. *Ruled out if* customers who fail rarely contacted you and their systems work. | Fix service and payments, not sales |
| **A new alternative in the area** (grid connection, a competitor's offer). | Churn concentrated in particular areas, and customers say they switched. *Ruled out if* churn is spread evenly. | Target elsewhere; respond on product or price |
| **The board's view: 40% is the price of scale.** | Each monthly group's collections cover total cost with room to spare. The newest groups and channels do as well as older ones, and cost per customer held steady when spend rose. *Ruled out if* the groups lose money or are getting worse. | Double, but into the segments that pay back |

## Recommendation: earn the doubling

There are three realistic options:

- **Double now.** Fastest sales growth, but you scale economics you haven't measured and that are probably negative for the extra customers.
- **Freeze spend until churn is fixed.** Protects cash, but stalls growth, puts your agent network at risk and teaches you nothing about scaling.
- **Grow in stages.** Fix and scale at the same time, and let spend follow the evidence.

**I recommend growing in stages.** You give up some sales growth next quarter. In return you learn what the next shilling buys before you spend it at scale.

### 1. Analyse existing customers by sign-up month: weeks 0–4 (CFO, with credit and analytics)

Your payment platform should already hold the data, so this is analysis, not new research. Take each month's new customers over the last 12–18 months. Split them by agent or distributor, region, product, deposit level and sales channel, and measure:

- status at 30, 60, 90 and 180 days, using the four groups above
- total collections so far as a share of contract value, at each of those points
- full upfront cost, and what repossessed or refurbished units recover
- the break-even failure rate and the maximum affordable acquisition cost for each segment
- the trend: are newer customers better or worse than older ones? If spend has risen before, what did the extra spend buy?

The result is a map of segments that make money (scale them), are marginal (fix them) or lose money (stop or redesign). Also find which early sign best predicted 90-day failure in past groups, such as a missed first payment or days paid in the first month. That early sign lets you manage without waiting three months for results.

### 2. Test fixes with control groups: from week 2

Assign agents or districts at random to new ways of working or business as usual. The times below are starting points to agree with operations.

- **Agent pay.** Pay part of the commission at installation and the rest at repayment milestones, such as a run of on-time payments or reaching day 90. Take commission back if the customer misses the first payment. Watch sales per agent and agent turnover, because competitors who pay full commission at installation may poach your best agents.
- **Check-in call within about 48 hours of installation.** Confirm the system works and that the customer knows the instalment, the total cost, how to pay and what happens after a missed payment. Test the Kiswahili scripts with local customer-care staff and customers.
- **Contact within a day or two of a first missed payment.** Offer to reschedule or pause, rather than locking the system and forgetting the customer.
- **Screening before sale.** A minimum deposit or a short scoring checklist in the segments the analysis flags.
- **Seasonal plans,** only in regions where the data show seasonality. Don't assume one national pattern.
- **Faster repairs,** if faults show up before customers stop paying.

**Main measure:** 90-day collections for each shilling of acquisition spend. **Early warning:** missed first payments. **Also watch:** total sales, agent turnover and complaints. Keep any change that raises collections per shilling, even if sales dip.

### 3. Release the extra spend in stages: from about week 6

- **Stage 1:** a limited increase, sized with the CFO against how long your cash lasts (+20–30% is a sensible opening figure), spent only in segments that already pay back. Run it as a comparison: extra spend in some districts, none in similar ones. The board then sees what the extra customers cost and how often they fail, not just averages.
- **Later stages** unlock when new customers pass targets agreed in advance. The early warning sign should be comfortably below the break-even failure rate. The cost of extra customers should stay under the segment's ceiling. There should be enough cash and loan-covenant headroom.
- **Stop rule:** if early signs from a stage point above break-even, pause that stage. Don't wait for day 90.

## How to put it to the board

- **Agree with the ambition, but change what's counted.** Report customers still paying at 90 days, and collections per shilling of acquisition spend, alongside total sales. Today's 8,000 is really about 4,800.
- **Ask for approval** of the customer analysis, the tests and stage 1. Keep doubling as the stated goal, released as each stage passes its targets.
- **Name the risk of skipping this step.** The loan book grows faster than it's repaid, and repossessions happen in plain view of the communities you sell to. If referrals drive your sales, check whether areas with many failed customers later sell less.

## What would change my advice

I'd support doubling sooner, into the right segments, if the analysis shows four things:

- collections from each whole monthly group, including failed customers, comfortably exceed total cost after financing
- recent groups perform no worse than older ones
- cost per customer held steady the last time spend rose
- much of the 40% is customers who pause and come back
