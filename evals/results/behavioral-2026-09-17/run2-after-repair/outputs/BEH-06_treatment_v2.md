# Gen Z beer persona: Dar es Salaam

**Before you use this, three things change how it should be used:**

1. **Legal-age Gen Z only, built around 25+.** Gen Z (born roughly 1997–2012) is about 14 to 29 years old today. Everyone born after mid-September 2008 is under Tanzania's legal drinking age of 18, and a beer brand can't target, show or knowingly reach them. Brewer marketing codes usually go further. They require mostly legal-age audiences and people in ads who are, and look, 25 or older. I haven't checked the law or your company's code for this, so confirm both with legal. That's why the persona below is 25.

2. **It's a hypothesis, not research.** You haven't given me any Dar es Salaam consumer, sales or media data, and I didn't search for any. Everything below is informed judgement for your team to test. **I wrote the quotes to bring the character to life. Real consumers didn't say them, so don't show them as verbatims in a deck.** No Dar es Salaam native speaker has reviewed the Kiswahili.

3. **"Gen Z are the future" is only half a targeting case.** You do need to recruit each new group of legal-age drinkers, because repeat-purchase brands like beer usually grow mainly by winning more buyers. That doesn't make young adults *the* target, though. Two numbers from your data decide it:
   - How much of your volume comes from drinkers over 30?
   - Is your brand's share among drinkers aged 18–29 lower than among older drinkers?

   If it is lower, recruiting young drinkers is a real problem and this persona deserves priority. If it isn't, you risk paying to chase a group you already win while the drinkers who pay the bills drift away. **My suggested stance:** make legal-age young adults a priority *recruitment* audience inside a brand that still speaks to all adult beer drinkers, not the only target.

## Baraka Joseph, 25: "the round-builder"
*Working persona. Every line is a hypothesis to validate.*

### Snapshot

| | |
|---|---|
| **Age and life stage** | 25, three years into working life, single and dating |
| **Home** | Shares a rented place in Sinza with a cousin (area is illustrative) |
| **Work** | Customer-service officer on a fixed-term contract. On the side, resells phone accessories through WhatsApp and Instagram |
| **Money** | Irregular. Comfortable the week after payday, tight by month-end. Sends money home towards younger siblings' school costs |
| **Phone** | Mid-range Android. Buys data in bundles and watches what it costs; uses Wi-Fi at work when he can |
| **Payment** | Cash or mobile money, depending on the outlet |

### Where beer fits in his life
He's past his first drinks and is now settling on a **repertoire**: two to four beers he rotates, plus a spirit with a mixer or a ready-to-drink on big nights. He isn't loyal to one brand. What he orders depends on the occasion, the table, what's cold and what's left in his pocket. At this stage a brand either earns a place in his repertoire or doesn't. That is the commercial reason to target him: winning new buyers (penetration), not deepening loyalty.

**Occasions, in likely order of importance (check the order):**
1. **Friday or Saturday with the washkaji** (his friends) at a neighbourhood bar: rounds, nyama choma, the group ordering together.
2. **Match nights:** Premier League weekends and Simba–Yanga, watched at a bar with a screen.
3. **Celebrations:** birthdays, send-offs and graduations, where someone else often buys the first rounds.
4. **The "proper" night out,** about once a month at a lounge in Masaki or Mikocheni. Here what's in his hand is seen and photographed.
5. **A quiet beer on a weeknight:** rare, and very price-sensitive.

**What decides his order at the counter:** whether it's cold; what the first person at the table ordered; the price per bottle against tonight's budget; how it looks in his hand on a lounge night; and how light it is, because he has work on Monday.

**His alternatives:** other beers; spirits with mixers and ready-to-drinks, especially at lounges; soft or energy drinks, or not drinking, when money or mood says no.

### Motivations
1. **Belonging.** Beer is his share of the round, the match and the table. *Brand role:* be the easy default the group orders.
2. **Showing progress on a tight budget.** He wants to look like he's moving up, especially on nights when he's seen. *Brand role:* look credible at the lounge without pricing him out on an ordinary Friday.
3. **Staying in control.** Rent, family obligations and a contract job mean he wants to enjoy the night and still turn up on Monday. *Brand role:* offer lighter options and a tone that respects him without lecturing.

**Core tension (test this one hardest):** he wants to be seen as generous and doing well, but his income can't support that every week. He copes by choosing *where* and *when* to spend: a cheap local bar most weeks, one night out where he's seen each month. If that's true, the brand has to win in two very different outlets at two very different prices.

**What he wants from a beer brand:** be there, and cold, where he actually goes. Show up inside things he already loves (football, music, friends) instead of interrupting them. Feel current without imitating how he talks. Don't treat him like a kid.

## What this means for the brand, if the persona holds
The job is to **get into young legal-age drinkers' repertoires** (penetration). Four things have to go right:

1. **Be the default order in his main occasions.** Tie the brand to "Friday with the washkaji" and "match night". Measure whether the brand comes to mind in those moments, not general awareness.
2. **Be cold and visible along his route.** Map the bars and lounges young adults use in areas such as Sinza, Kinondoni, Mikocheni and Masaki, then check cold stock and visibility outlet by outlet. It's operational work, but it may be the biggest lever.
3. **Suit his budget per round and at month-end.** Compare your shelf price with the other beers on the table, both at the cheap local bar and at the lounge.
4. **Earn a place on the nights he's seen,** through pack design and presence in lounges. Prove this works before reaching for a broad "youthful" repositioning.

**What not to do:** no campus or school-leaver activity; no creators under 25 or with mostly young audiences; don't drop older drinkers from your communications.

**Speculative option:** if research shows that many young adults in Dar don't drink, for religious or personal reasons, an alcohol-free extension could put the brand in their occasions too. Only pursue it if the evidence supports it.

## Turning this into evidence quickly
1. **Start with your own data.** From any tracker, usage-and-attitudes study or sales data you have, pull volume and brand penetration by age band, and your share among drinkers aged 18–29 against those over 30. This decides whether the persona deserves priority.
2. **Qualitative research.** Run small friendship groups with legal-age drinkers aged 18–29, including people who don't drink beer. Cover two or three areas of Dar and a range of incomes, split by gender, and watch Friday nights and match nights in bars. This tests the occasions, motivations, core tension and how they really talk. A sensible starting point is 6–8 groups; agree the number with your research agency.
3. **Media.** Run a short, age-verified survey of legal-age young adults in Dar on which platforms and radio stations they use, and when. Start with about 300–400 people and agree the size with your agency. Before booking any account or creator, get the platform's data on audience ages. Don't read national mobile or internet subscription counts as reach: TCRA counts SIMs, not people.
4. **Rewrite the persona** once results are in. Replace each hypothesis with a finding, or delete it.

**What would break this persona:**
- **Young Dar drinkers mostly start with spirits or ready-to-drinks.** The job then becomes bringing them into beer at all, not just winning share within beer.
- **Young women make up a large share of new drinkers.** One male persona would then point the brand the wrong way, and you'd need a second persona.
- **Your brand already wins its fair share of young drinkers.** The "future" argument then doesn't justify moving money.

Also, don't import the "Gen Z drink less" story from Europe or the US. Nobody has tested it for Dar.
