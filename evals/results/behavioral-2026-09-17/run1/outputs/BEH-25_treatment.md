## Why there's no percentage

- **The sample doesn't represent Tanzanian parents.** There are six interviews, all with parents who have a child in a private primary school, all in Dodoma or Dar es Salaam. That's exploratory work, not a measure of parents in general.
- **Even a count would mislead.** Four of the six mention paying by phone in some way. If that were a random sample, "4 of 6" would fit a true share anywhere from about 30% to 90%. And it isn't a random sample.
- **Preference wasn't measured.** Most parents describe what they *do*. Only one states a preference, and it's for paying in person.

**Counts (this sample only):**
- Said they prefer mobile money: 0 of 6.
- Stated any preference: 1 of 6, for paying in person.
- Pay by phone: 2 clearly (P1, P3), 1 only when she has balance (P5), and 1 probably, with help (P6).

**What the interviews can support:** for several of these parents, paying by phone is one of the ways they pay. It comes with friction: the school's receipt rules, charges, cash flow, wanting to see the payment made, and needing help. These are leads worth sizing. They don't tell you how common any of this is.

A headline these interviews can carry: *"In six early interviews, parents described paying fees by phone, and the receipts, charges and cash-flow gaps that get in the way."*

## Three quotes for the deck

I picked these to show the range. Three happy phone users on one slide would overstate the evidence.

**1. Adoption is happening, but the school's process hasn't caught up**
> "Last time I took the money to the bank, but now I pay by phone. But the school wants a bank receipt."
> *"Nilipeleka pesa benki mara ya mwisho, lakini sasa nalipa kwa simu. Ila shule inataka risiti ya benki."*
> Mother, Dodoma

**2. The limit may be cash flow, not the payment channel**
> "It depends. If I have balance, phone. If not, I wait."
> *"Inategemea. Kama nina salio, simu. Kama sina, nasubiri."*
> Mother, Dar es Salaam

**3. The counter-voice**
> "I prefer to pay at the school myself so that I can see."
> *"Napendelea kulipa mwenyewe shuleni ili nione."*
> Father, Dodoma

**Swap-in:** if the slide is about instalments, use P3: "I pay little by little every month by phone." **Avoid P2.** The quote stops at "But the charges...", so the audience will guess what came next.

**Before these go in the deck:**
- **Check the translations.** The moderator translated the interviews and nobody checked them. Get a native speaker to review, and keep the Kiswahili on the slide or in the notes.
- **Don't caption past what was said.** Three quotes are open to more than one reading:
  - **P4:** nobody asked what "ili nione" ("so that I can see") means. It could be proof of payment, seeing the school, or not trusting phone transfers. Don't caption it "distrust of mobile money."
  - **P5:** "salio" could mean her mobile-money balance or money in general. "Nasubiri" ("I wait") probably means she pays late, not that she switches to another method.
  - **P1:** we don't know whether she still ends up going to the bank for the receipt.

## If the deck needs a number

1. **Best: payment records.** Get fee-payment data from schools or a school-payment provider, showing the share of payments by channel. That measures what parents do, not what they say.
2. **Or a survey.** First decide who counts: private-school parents only? Which regions? Then ask three things separately: how they paid last time, how they'd prefer to pay, and what gets in the way. About 400 completed interviews for each group you want to report gives roughly ±5 points (95% confidence, random sample).
3. **Cheap interim step: call back these six.** Ask about P1's receipt, P2's charges, what P4 wants to see, what P5 does when she "waits," and how P6's payment is sent. That sharpens the survey questions but still won't give you a percentage.
