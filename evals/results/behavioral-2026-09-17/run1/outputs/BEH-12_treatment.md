## The short answer

This is an availability problem caused by the warehouse change, not a demand problem. People buy your biscuits just as fast as before wherever they can find them. You're losing the sales at empty shelves.

The "insight" is about operations, and that's fine. You don't need a consumer tension to justify fixing it. What it means is simple: **fix supply to the shelf before you spend a shilling on advertising, promotions or price.**

**How sure am I?** I'm highly confident that availability is driving the loss in your top 500 outlets. I'm only moderately confident that it explains the whole 10%. Two quick checks below will settle that.

## Why the data points this way

- **The flat rate of sale where you're stocked is the giveaway.** If the cause were price, a competitor, a shrinking category or a weaker brand, shoppers would buy less even where your product is on the shelf. They don't. The only explanation that fits all three facts is that you've lost chances to sell.
- **Timing.** The jump in out-of-stocks came after the warehouse change. That's how you describe it, so check that the weeks match the volume drop.
- **The numbers roughly add up.** In your top 500, the share of outlets with stock fell from 95% to 81%. With rate of sale unchanged, that cuts **about 15% of top-500 volume** (0.81 ÷ 0.95 = 0.85). Whether that covers your full 10% depends on how much of your total volume the top 500 sell:

| Top 500 share of total volume | Loss explained by their out-of-stocks | Loss left unexplained | What it suggests |
|---|---|---|---|
| ~40% | ~6 pts | ~4 pts | Some of the loss is outside the top 500: most likely the same supply failure in outlets you don't audit, or a second cause |
| ~50% | ~7 pts | ~3 pts | Same as above, but a smaller gap |
| ~68% | ~10 pts | None | Availability explains the whole drop |
| ~80% | ~12 pts | Explains ~2 pts more than you lost | Some sales come back: shoppers wait, or buy another of your SKUs instead |

This assumes the out-of-stock figure roughly equals the share of selling time lost, and that the top-500 list was the same before and after.

## What could prove this wrong: check these first (days, not weeks)

1. **Compare outlets that stayed in stock with outlets that ran out.** Split the top 500 into those two groups. If the loss sits in the outlets that ran out and the others are flat, the diagnosis holds. If outlets that stayed in stock also lost volume, demand changed too, so look again at price, competitors and the category. Compare rate of sale in the same outlets before and after. Don't use the average across whichever outlets happen to have stock, because that group keeps changing.
2. **Definitions.**
   - Is "volume" what you ship to the trade (sell-in) or what outlets sell to shoppers (sell-out)? If it's sell-in, part of the 10% may be distributors and wholesalers running down their stock during the move, which will bounce back. Or stock may be stuck in the new system, which won't.
   - Was out-of-stock measured on the same outlets and SKUs, with the same audit method, before and after?
   - Does "out of stock" mean the whole brand is missing, or just one SKU? If it's any SKU, some shoppers will have bought another of yours.
3. **Other changes at the same time.** Did anything else change in those weeks? Look for a new price list, SKUs cut as part of the move, a competitor launch or trade deal, or seasonal swings. Check whether competitors gained share in the outlets where you ran out.

## Find where the supply chain breaks

An empty shelf can have four different causes, each with a different fix. Compare each measure before and after the move:

| Where it breaks | Data to check | Fix if this is the cause |
|---|---|---|
| Stock isn't in the new warehouse | Weeks of stock on hand, and the share of each SKU's orders the warehouse can fill | More safety stock on the few SKUs that sell most; fix restocking and forecasts from factory to warehouse |
| Stock is there, but orders go out short or wrong | Share of each order delivered, on-time-in-full deliveries to distributors and outlets, errors in product codes and records | Clean up product records; pick top SKUs first; hold the warehouse operator to a service agreement |
| Deliveries arrive later or less often | Time from order to delivery, deliveries per outlet per month, route changes | Deliver to top outlets as often as before; re-plan routes; keep buffer stock with distributors or at a depot closer to them |
| Orders aren't being placed | Orders per outlet, order size, whether sales reps still visit on schedule, credit terms | Restore rep visit schedules and suggested orders; check whether credit or minimum-order rules changed |

Split the results by region and by how stock reaches the outlet: direct delivery, distributor, wholesaler or van sales. A new warehouse location changes delivery times differently on each route, so a national average can hide a few broken routes.

If some of your top 500 are wholesalers supplying dukas, their out-of-stocks pass on to shops you don't audit. That would also explain any loss left unexplained in the first table.

## What to do

**Now (next two weeks): stop the losses**
- Put one person in charge across supply chain and sales. Give them a daily view of out-of-stocks in the top 500, by SKU and route.
- Rank the top 500 by volume and restock the biggest outlets first, by direct delivery, van sales or extra stock held with distributors. Each point of out-of-stock costs most there.
- Protect the few SKUs that sell most: give them extra safety stock and pick them first.
- If the old warehouse or a distributor can still ship, use it for the worst routes until the new warehouse meets its targets.
- **Hold** consumer promotions, price cuts and extra advertising in affected areas. None of them fills a shelf, and promotions can empty shelves faster. Unless the checks above point back to demand, this evidence doesn't call for a brand or advertising review.

**Next (weeks 2 to 8): fix the cause and keep it fixed**
- Fix whichever break point the table above identifies.
- Agree targets with the warehouse by a fixed date:
  - share of each order delivered
  - on-time-in-full deliveries
  - time from order to delivery
  - out-of-stocks back to your pre-move 5%
- Extend the out-of-stock audit to a sample of outlets outside the top 500, so you can see whether the damage goes wider.

**Then: check for damage that lasts after the fix**

Once shelves are full again, watch the outlets that were out of stock longest. If their rate of sale and shelf space go back to pre-move levels, you're done. If not, shopkeepers may have given your space to a competitor, or shoppers may have switched brands. For now that's only a possibility; your data doesn't show it. Only at that point is it worth paying to win outlets back, through shelf-space recovery, shopkeeper visits or in-store activity. Test that in some of those outlets against a comparison group before rolling it out.

## What it's worth

- Each point of out-of-stock in the top 500 costs about 1% of their pre-move volume.
- Getting from 19% back to 5% would lift their current volume by about 17%.
- **Weekly margin at risk** = pre-move weekly top-500 volume × 15% × profit per unit. That's also the most worth spending each week on emergency logistics while the fix beds in.

## Decision rules

- **Diagnosis confirmed:** the loss sits in outlets that ran out of stock, and outlets that stayed in stock are flat.
- **Look again at demand:** outlets that stayed in stock are also declining, or competitors are gaining where you never ran out.
- **Start winning back outlets and shoppers:** out-of-stocks have been close to 5% for several weeks (say four), but top-500 volume hasn't recovered most of its ~15% gap.
