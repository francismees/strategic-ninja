# Don't double acquisition spend yet. Fix the 90-day leak first, then release the budget in stages.

## Short answer

Say yes to growth and no to doubling spend now. If 40% of customers are gone within 90 days, you're paying to acquire about **3,200 customers a month (roughly 38,000 a year)** who get hardware, installation and an agent commission, and give back little more than a deposit.

In PAYGo, the sale doesn't make you money. The payments do. Doubling spend on the current engine will most likely:

- double the capital you tie up,
- bring in worse customers at the margin,
- put your debt facilities at risk before it adds much profit.

Propose this instead: keep spend flat for one quarter, find and fix what's causing the early losses, then release the extra budget in tranches as each group of new customers shows it pays.

---

## Why doubling now probably destroys value

### 1. You're probably close to breaking even on each customer

The figures below are **placeholders**. Swap in your own; the logic holds either way.

- **Upfront cost per customer:** $100 ($70 for hardware, logistics and installation, plus $30 for acquisition and agent commission)
- **Contract:** $15 deposit, then $10 a month for 18 months
- **Customer who stays:** pays about 90% of the contract. After servicing and financing costs, that's about **+$50**.
- **Customer lost within 90 days:** pays the deposit plus about 6 weeks of payments ($30). You recover and refurbish half the units, worth about $10 net. That's about **−$65**.

On these numbers you break even when **about 43%** of customers are lost early. You're at 40%.

| Scenario | New customers/month | 90-day loss | Monthly contribution | Net capital deployed/month |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Today | 8,000 | 40% | **+$32k** | $680k |
| Double spend, quality holds (optimistic) | 16,000 | 40% | +$64k | $1.36M |
| Double spend, the extra customers lose 50% (more likely) | 16,000 | 40% on the first 8,000, 50% on the extra | **−$28k** | $1.36M |
| Same spend, loss cut to 25% | 8,000 | 25% | **+$170k** | $680k |
| Cut loss to 25%, then double (extra at 30%) | 16,000 | 25% on the first 8,000, 30% on the extra | **+$294k** | $1.36M |

**What the table shows:**
- Cutting early loss to 25% at today's spend earns over 2.5 times as much as doubling spend, even if quality holds. It also uses half the capital.
- Each percentage point of early loss is worth about $1.15 per customer. That's roughly $9k a month, or $110k a year, at current volume.
- The doubling rows are generous. They assume twice the spend buys twice the customers, which it rarely does.

### 2. The next 8,000 customers will be worse than the first 8,000
Extra money goes to weaker agents, newer territories and less-qualified prospects. Agents paid on volume will find volume wherever it is. At the margin, acquisition cost rises and customer quality falls.

### 3. Operations will slip
Doubling volume strains installation, customer care, repairs and agent supervision. When service slips, customers stop paying.

### 4. Your lenders will notice before your P&L does
Receivables financing is sized and priced on collection rates and portfolio at risk. If the book deteriorates twice as fast, you risk breaching covenants, and those facilities fund your growth. Several East African PAYGo companies were forced to cut back or restructure in the late 2010s because growth outran collections.

### 5. The 60% who stay aren't all safe
More customers will default between months 4 and 18. The real loss rate is higher than 40%.

### 6. Losses spread by word of mouth
Every repossession or neighbour in default is a local advert against you. That pushes up future acquisition costs in exactly the villages you want to grow in.

## Step 1: Find out what the 40% really is (3 to 4 weeks)

**1. Split the number.** "Default or deactivate" usually mixes three groups, and each needs a different fix:
- **Never started:** paid the deposit, then little or nothing. Points to mis-selling, misunderstood terms or agent fraud.
- **Early drop:** paid for a few weeks, then stopped. Points to affordability, a broken or disappointing system, or a better alternative such as the grid.
- **Intermittent:** locks and unlocks. Usually seasonal cash flow, and many of these customers pay in the end. Don't write them off. Compare what they've paid so far with what the plan expected.

Report collection rate (days paid ÷ days elapsed) and 30/60/90-day repayment curves for each monthly sales cohort, not a single default figure.

**2. Check whether it gets worse as you grow.** Plot 90-day loss for each month's new customers over the last 18 to 24 months, next to sales volume. If losses rose as volume rose, that one chart answers the board.

**3. Find where it's concentrated.** Break it down by:
- agent
- district
- channel
- product
- deposit level
- month of sale
- customer's main income source

In sales-driven books, losses are usually concentrated in a minority of agents and territories. If yours are, moving existing budget away from them improves quality without losing much volume.

**4. Check for agent arbitrage.** If the upfront commission is bigger than the deposit, an agent can pay deposits on marginal or fake accounts and keep the difference. Look for:
- deposits paid from the same phone numbers,
- clusters of accounts that never make a second payment,
- agents whose customers are mostly "never started".

**5. Ask the customers who left why they stopped.** Have 200 to 300 of them called or visited in Swahili, by staff who are not collectors. Common reasons to listen for:
- couldn't afford it after an income shock
- the system underperformed, especially in the masika rains or because of the battery
- misunderstood the terms (thought the deposit bought the system, or was surprised by the lockout)
- the REA grid connection arrived
- payment friction: mobile money fees, agents without float, a changed SIM
- moved away, or the system was stolen
- felt pressured into buying

Also check month of sale. Customers who buy at harvest can hit the lean season or January school fees within their first 90 days.

## Step 2: The fixes that usually cut early losses most

1. **Pay agents for customers who pay.** This is usually the fastest and biggest lever.
   - Pay part of the commission at installation and the rest at day 60 to 90, only if the customer's payments are on track.
   - Claw back commission on "never started" accounts.
   - Rank agents on 90-day collection rate, not units sold. Retrain or drop the bottom tier.
   - Expect sales volume to dip for a while. That's the bad volume leaving.

2. **Screen and right-size at the point of sale.**
   - Put new or higher-risk customers on entry-level systems. Let them move up to TV kits after they've shown they pay.
   - Use the deposit as a filter: ask a higher deposit from higher-risk segments.
   - Add a simple scorecard. Where customers consent, use mobile money transaction data through operator partnerships, in line with the Personal Data Protection Act 2022.

3. **Own the first 30 days.** Whether a customer keeps paying is mostly settled in the first few weeks, and the first missed payment is your strongest warning.
   - Make a welcome call within 48 hours to confirm they understand price, term, lockout and ownership.
   - Show them how to pay via M-Pesa, Mixx by Yas, Airtel Money or HaloPesa.
   - Send an SMS *before* the system locks, allow a short grace period, and follow up on any lock lasting more than about 3 days.

4. **Match payments to rural cash flow.**
   - Offer weekly and monthly plans alongside daily ones.
   - Offer harvest-linked plans where income is seasonal: cashew in Mtwara and Lindi, cotton in the Lake Zone, maize and coffee in the Southern Highlands.
   - Let customers pay ahead at harvest and take planned pauses in lean months.
   - Price that flexibility into the contract rather than counting it as default.

5. **Fix service before chasing payments.** People stop paying for systems they think are broken.
   - Link fault tickets to the churn data.
   - Set deadlines for repairs and swaps.
   - Warn customers at the sale that output drops in the rainy season.

6. **Run recovery as a business.**
   - Escalate step by step: SMS, call, visit, restructure, and only then repossess.
   - Restructure the contract for customers willing to keep paying.
   - Refurbish recovered units and resell them as a cheaper entry product.

7. **Watch the grid.** Map REA's planned connections against your sales. In villages about to be connected, sell smaller systems or position solar as backup, not large kits on 18-month terms.

8. **Grow from the customers who pay.** The 60% who stay have already proven they pay. Sell them appliances and reward referrals. That growth costs less to acquire and comes with a payment history.

## Step 3: What to take back to the board

A staged plan with conditions, not a refusal:

| Phase | Timing | Spend | Actions | Gate to move on |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Diagnose and reallocate | Weeks 0–6 | Flat; move budget from the worst agents and territories to the best | Cohort analysis, exit calls, arbitrage check | Root causes quantified |
| 2. Test fixes | Months 2–4 | Flat, plus 10–15% of the proposed increase spent on fixes | Test commission redesign, onboarding, deposit and product rules, and flexible plans region by region against control groups | Test cohorts beat control groups on day-30 collections |
| 3. First scale-up | Months 4–6 | +30%, only into proven agents and channels | Scale what worked | Projected 90-day loss ≤ 30% |
| 4. Scale to 2x | Months 6–9+ | Up to +100% | Expand into new territories, with a stop-loss rule | 90-day loss ≤ 25%, and the extra customers keep that quality |

**Set the gates from your own break-even.** The 30% and 25% figures are placeholders.

**Don't wait 90 days to judge a cohort.** Use early signals: on-time first payment, share of days active in the first 30, and the "never started" rate at day 21. Check these against past cohorts to confirm they predict the 90-day outcome.

**Change the board dashboard:**
- "new customers" becomes "new customers still paying at day 90"
- cost per paying customer
- cohort collection rate
- share of the portfolio 30+ days overdue (PAR30)
- months to cash payback

**Add a stop-loss rule:** freeze the budget for any new channel or territory whose early signals point above break-even.

## When the board would be right

A faster ramp can make sense if your data show all of the following:
- 90-day loss is flat or falling as volume grows.
- Your true break-even is well above 40% (cheap devices, high deposits, strong resale of recovered units).
- The extra money has good places to go at stable acquisition cost.
- There's a time-limited reason to move, such as a results-based financing window or a competitor grabbing your best regions.

Even then, release the money in tranches with a stop-loss.

**Watch for one trap.** If results-based financing or carbon revenue is paid per unit sold, bad sales can look profitable. Check whether that income depends on systems staying in use, because carbon income usually does.
