# Tea business: checking the growth story before the board

## Short answer

**The headline holds up, but the growth story doesn't.** Net revenue did grow by double digits: up 12.2%, from TZS 1,720m to TZS 1,930m. But the file doesn't show a business selling more tea. The growth came from higher prices and a jump in the 250g pack. Total cartons fell 7.3%, two of the three packs shrank, gross margin fell 3.3 points and gross profit grew only 2.7% (TZS +18m).

I'd give the board a different story: **"Revenue grew 12% on price and the 250g pack. Now we need to fix unit margins and stop the smaller packs shrinking."** It's more believable than a plain growth story, and it answers the question someone will ask as soon as they see the margin line.

**Check this before anything else:** the file labels the periods "2025" and "2026", but it's only September 2026. If "2026" is a full financial year (for example July to June) of the same length as "2025", everything below holds. If it's year-to-date, a forecast or a period of a different length, the comparison needs redoing.

## Four points for the board

**1. Growth came from price and pack sizes, not from selling more cartons.**
Revenue bridge (TZS m): 1,720 in 2025, **+164 from higher prices**, +46 from changes in pack volumes at 2025 prices (250g +200, 50g −100, 100g −54), giving 1,930. Price accounts for about 78% of the increase.

**2. The 250g pack produced all of the growth, and more.**
The 250g pack added TZS 230m, more than the total increase. Its share of revenue went from 23% to 33%. The 50g and 100g packs together still make up two-thirds of revenue, and they shrank: revenue down 1.5%, gross profit down 10.8%.

**3. Margin fell on every pack, and the shift to 250g hid how much.**
Cost per carton rose 14.5–20%, while prices rose 5–12%. If each pack had kept its 2025 margin, the move towards 250g (the highest-margin pack) would have lifted gross margin to about 40.2%. Instead it fell to 36.1%.

Gross profit bridge (TZS m):
- Higher prices: +164
- Higher unit costs: −179
- Pack volumes and mix: +33
- Net change: +18

Per carton, cost rose more than price on 50g (+650 vs +600) and on 250g (+1,600 vs +1,000). On 100g, price covered the extra cost in shillings (+1,000 vs +897) but not in percentage terms.

**4. After inflation, growth looks flatter.**
Mainland headline inflation was 4.3% (NBS consumer price index, August 2026 vs August 2025). As a rough adjustment, revenue grew about 7–8% in real terms and gross profit fell about 1.5%. Treat this as indicative only: the index period doesn't match yours, and household prices are not your selling prices or costs. It fits better as a spoken caveat or footnote than as a headline.

## Gaps in the file that could change the story

- **Volume in tonnes.** Cartons of 50g, 100g and 250g packs can't simply be added together.
  - If every carton holds the same number of packs, tea by weight actually rose about 5% even though cartons fell.
  - If cartons hold roughly the same weight, it fell about 7%.

  Get the packs-per-carton figures before anyone tells the board "volume fell." The revenue and margin findings don't depend on this.
- **Why 250g jumped 50%.** The data can't tell these explanations apart:
  - (a) Buyers moving up from smaller packs: 250g had the smallest price rise, so it got cheaper relative to the others.
  - (b) A new listing, channel or large customer, such as an institution or wholesaler.
  - (c) Distributors stocking up rather than shoppers buying more.

  Rule out (c) first. If it is stock-building, next year's comparison will suffer. Check distributor stock levels, sales out of distributors, and whether the 250g increase is concentrated in a few customers.
- **Why 50g lost a sixth of its cartons.** It had the biggest price rise (+12%). Buyers reacting to price, a move to bigger packs, lost shelf presence and competitors are all possible. If 50g is your entry pack or small-shop pack, the loss may be buyers rather than just cartons, but sales totals can't show that.
- **Why cost per carton rose 15–20%.** The file doesn't break COGS down (tea leaf, packaging, freight, exchange rate, one-offs).
- **What "net revenue" deducts, and profit after marketing and trade spend.** If discounts or spend were raised to push 250g, profit after that spend grew by less than gross profit did.

## Suggested board storyline

Each slide title states its point, so the titles read as the argument on their own:

1. Net revenue grew 12% to TZS 1,930m, led by price increases and strong 250g growth.
2. Price added TZS 164m, and more 250g cartons (+TZS 200m) more than offset fewer 50g and 100g cartons (−TZS 154m).
3. Gross profit grew only 3% because unit costs outpaced price on every pack; gross margin fell 3.3 points to 36%.
4. The 50g and 100g packs, two-thirds of revenue, are shrinking; we need to know why before raising prices again.
5. Plan for next year: recover unit margin, stabilise the 50g and 100g packs, and confirm the 250g growth reflects real consumer demand.
6. Ask: the board notes the results and endorses a margin and core-volume review, reporting back at [next quarterly board].

**Why not lead with "double-digit growth"?** The margin and carton figures will come out anyway, and the story loses credibility when they do. Leading with the plain growth story also hides the two problems that need a decision.

**Why not delay?** The revenue and margin findings don't depend on the open questions. The only thing that must be confirmed first is what period "2026" covers.

## Priorities to propose (subject to the checks above)

- **Recover margin.** Break the cost-per-carton increase down and separate lasting cost rises from one-offs. On 250g, price rose 5% against a 14.5% cost rise. Test a modest increase in a few territories against comparable ones before rolling it out, because 250g's relative value may be what's driving its growth.
- **Stabilise the 50g and 100g packs.** Find where their cartons were lost: which region, channel and customer, and whether fewer shops stock them or each shop sells less. Do this before choosing a price, pack or distribution response.
- **Confirm 250g growth will last.** Check sales out of distributors against shipments to them, stock levels and customer concentration.

**For next quarter's board, track by pack:** cartons and tonnes, net price and COGS per carton, gross margin, 250g sales in versus sales out, and 50g distribution if you measure it.

**What would change the story:**
- If most of the 250g growth turns out to be distributors stocking up, restate it as a timing effect, and the plan needs to focus harder on the smaller packs.
- If tonnage rose, "volume fell" becomes "shoppers bought more tea in bigger packs." That's a better story, but the margin problem remains.
