# Don't double acquisition spend yet. Fix what the spend buys, then scale in stages.

**My advice:** Don't approve a blanket doubling now. Ask the board to approve growth in stages instead:

- **Analysis:** 4–6 weeks of cohort analysis using data you already hold.
- **Immediate fixes:** changes to how sales are made and how new customers are onboarded.
- **Staged spend:** extra acquisition money released in tranches. Each tranche goes only to the channels, agents and regions whose recent customers are actually paying.

The ambition to grow stays. What changes is the order of the work, and what you count as growth: customers still paying at day 90, not activations.

This is a conditional recommendation. Your own repayment data could show the board is right, and section 7 says what that would look like.

## 1. The problem, restated

- **Objective:** profitable growth of the customer base and the book of customer payments owed to you. I'm assuming this. Confirm what the board is actually aiming for: new sign-ups, revenue, market share or a fundraising milestone. Each one changes the right answer.
- **Symptom:** 40% of new customers default or deactivate within three months. This is your company's own figure; I read it as 40% of each monthly cohort.
- **Proposed solution:** double acquisition spend.
- **Missing link:** whether the *next* shilling of acquisition spend brings in customers who pay more than they cost.

At today's rate, 8,000 new customers a month means about 3,200 lost within three months and about 4,800 still paying. So each customer still paying at day 90 costs 1.67 times the headline acquisition cost per sale.

In pay-as-you-go (PAYG), every sale is also hardware you've paid for up front. Doubling acquisition roughly doubles the money you put into devices each month (at a similar product mix). Today, 40% of that money ends up with customers who stop paying early.

## 2. Why the average hides the real decision

The board is probably reasoning from the average customer. The decision is really about the *marginal* customer, meaning the extra customers the extra money brings in.

When you double spend, those extra customers usually come from harder-to-reach pools: newer or overstretched agents, weaker territories, promotions and lower-income households. They often cost more to win and repay worse. This pattern is common, but for your business it's a hypothesis you can check against your own history.

Here is an illustration with made-up round numbers. They form an index, not your data:

- Device plus installation costs 30 per customer.
- Acquisition costs 20.
- A customer still paying at month 3 eventually pays 100 in total.
- A customer lost in the first three months brings in 15 (deposit, early payments, and device recovery after its costs).

| Scenario (one monthly cohort) | New customers | Value per customer (payments minus costs) | Cohort value | vs today |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Today (40% lost in first 3 months) | 8,000 | 16 | 128,000 | – |
| Double spend; extra customers behave like today's | 16,000 | 16 | 256,000 | +100% |
| Double spend; extra customers cost 50% more to acquire and 55% are lost early | ~13,300 | ~6.9 average (each extra customer: −6.75) | ~92,000 | −28% |
| Keep spend flat; cut early loss to 30% (onboarding adds 2 per customer) | 8,000 | 22.5 | 180,000 | +41% |

Three points from this:

1. **The second and third rows are the best and worst case for the board's plan.** In the worst case, new sign-ups grow 67% while the cohort's value falls. A dashboard of averages would not show it. Your repayment history tells you which row you are in.
2. **In this illustration the average customer is still profitable at 40% early loss.** Break-even is about 59%. That's exactly why the average reassures people and can still mislead. Build this model with your real costs and payments.
3. **Fixing retention is a lever of similar size that needs no extra capital.** The two levers multiply each other: once cohorts keep paying for longer, every later increase in spend is worth more.

## 3. What is driving the 40%? Competing explanations

The "40% default or deactivate" figure probably combines problems that need different fixes. Several of them can be true at once. The job is to find which one limits you most, and how much of the 40% each accounts for.

**1. Sales quality.** Agents are paid at sale, so they sign up people who can't or won't pay, or who don't understand the terms. This can include fake or recycled accounts.
- *You'd see:* defaults concentrated among a minority of agents or in promotion periods; many customers never paying after the deposit; stops in the first weeks; many defaulters unreachable or unaware of the lockout.
- *Ruled out if:* defaults are spread evenly across agents, and stops come late, after regular payments.
- *For the board:* fix commissions and point-of-sale checks before scaling. Spend can still grow through proven agents now.

**2. Affordability or plan fit.** Payment schedules don't match household cash flow.
- *You'd see:* irregular payments before the final stop; clustering by region and month of sale (possibly farming-season effects, to be tested rather than assumed); more failures on larger kits or shorter terms.
- *Ruled out if:* customers paid steadily and then stopped abruptly, with no regional or seasonal pattern.
- *For the board:* redesign the offer (entry kit, longer terms, flexible or seasonal plans) and tighten screening. More spend without this amplifies losses.

**3. Product or after-sales failure.**
- *You'd see:* fault reports or failed installations *before* the non-payment; clusters by product batch, installer or area; faulty units among repossessed devices.
- *Ruled out if:* defaulters rarely reported faults and recovered devices work.
- *For the board:* stop scaling the affected products or areas until fixed. More marketing would spread bad word of mouth.

**4. Payment friction.** Customers want to pay but can't easily.
- *You'd see:* failed payments, errors entering unlock codes, changed or shared phone numbers; customers resuming payment once contacted.
- *Ruled out if:* defaulters still don't pay after contact, and there is no trail of failed transactions.
- *For the board:* fix the payment and reminder process. This is cheap, fast and low risk.

**5. Customers leaving for something else** (grid connection, a competitor, buying a system outright).
- *You'd see:* losses clustered in newly electrified or competitor-heavy areas, and customers saying so.
- *Ruled out if:* there's no geographic clustering, and the reasons customers give are money or the product.
- *For the board:* change where you target. Less of the problem is fixable.

**6. The metric overstates the loss.** "Deactivated" includes customers whose devices are temporarily locked for non-payment and who later start paying again.
- *You'd see:* many accounts locked at day 90 that resume later, with meaningful total payments from them.
- *Ruled out if:* few locked accounts resume and they pay little afterwards.
- *For the board:* the problem is smaller than 40%. Work on getting locked customers paying again, and scale faster.

## 4. The analysis to run: 4–6 weeks, mostly with data you already have

A PAYG platform normally records every sale and payment, every device lock and unlock, and the agent, region, product and plan behind each account. If yours does, most of this is quick:

1. **Break down the 40%.** Split it into customers who never paid after the deposit; paid, then stopped; were locked but later resumed; had the device repossessed; or returned it faulty. Measure what "deactivated" accounts go on to pay after day 90.
2. **Track loss by month of sale** for the last 12–24 months. The key test is whether the share lost by day 90 rose as sales volume rose. If it did, doubling will probably make it worse. Before reading that as cause and effect, check for other changes over the same period (price, product, new regions).
3. **See where losses concentrate.** Cut the day-90 loss by agent, sales channel, region, product, plan and deposit level, and by promotion vs non-promotion periods. Treat Mainland regions and Zanzibar separately if you operate in both. Losses concentrated in a few places point to sales quality; losses spread evenly point to the offer or the product.
4. **Check timing.** Count the days from sale to the first missed payment. Very early stops point to sales quality or installation; stops at 6–12 weeks point to affordability or service.
5. **Check what extra spend actually bought.** Compare customers from your most recent expansions (new agents, new regions, promotions) with your established base. This is the closest evidence you have for what doubling would buy.
6. **Ask the defaulters.** Make structured calls, in Kiswahili, to a random sample of a few hundred defaulted customers. Include the hard-to-reach ones and record who can't be reached, since that may point to fake sales. Visit a subset to confirm the device exists and works. Sort the reasons against the six explanations above.
7. **Build the unit economics model** from section 2 with real numbers. Calculate two figures:
   - the early-loss rate at which a customer stops being profitable;
   - the most you can pay to acquire an extra customer, given the loss rate your expansion customers actually show.

## 5. The options for the board

**A. Double spend now.**
- *Gain:* volume and scale.
- *Risk:* the extra customers destroy value; money is tied up in devices held by non-payers; after-sales support gets overloaded; lenders and investors see a weaker book of customer payments.
- *View:* right only if the extra customers behave like today's, and that isn't known yet.

**B. Freeze spend and fix retention first.**
- *Gain:* retention and collections.
- *Risk:* lost momentum and agents; competitors take territory; fixed costs are spread over fewer sales.
- *View:* too cautious if some channels are already performing well.

**C. Fix, then scale in tranches with release rules (recommended).**
- *Gain:* retention first, then volume where it's already proven to pay.
- *Risk:* slower growth in new sign-ups, and the release rules need discipline.
- *View:* grows paying customers and shows which spend works.

**Not now:**
- A broad awareness campaign. Nothing so far suggests awareness is what's holding growth back.
- Blanket deposit discounts or promotions to drive volume. Check how customers from past promotions performed first.
- Expansion into new regions before you understand how existing customers perform.

## 6. The recommended plan

**Do immediately (low risk, whatever the diagnosis):**
- **A welcome call or visit within the first days of sale.** Confirm the customer exists, understands the daily price, the lock rules and how to pay, and knows who to call about faults. Test the Kiswahili script with target customers to make sure it's understood. This targets fake sales, mis-selling and payment friction.
- **Contact at the first missed payment.** Offer ways back into good standing (a short pause, a new schedule) before any move to repossession. Track how many customers resume paying.
- **Clean contact data.** Changed or shared numbers mean reminders don't arrive. Take a second contact at sale.

**Pilot, with comparison groups (from month 1–2):**
- **Change how agents are paid.** Pay part of the commission at sale and part when the customer reaches set payment milestones, and claw it back if the customer defaults early. Roll it out region by region, or compare regions matched on past performance, so the effect can be separated from seasonality. Keep total pay competitive for good agents, because competitors paying everything at sale may try to poach them.
- **Redesign the offer** where affordability is the problem: an entry kit, longer terms or flexible schedules, in the regions where the analysis shows cash-flow patterns. Judge it on total payments over the customer's life, not just on lower default.
- **Referrals from customers who pay reliably** as an acquisition channel. The hypothesis is that it brings in better-vetted customers at lower cost. Test it against customers recruited by agents.

**Release acquisition spend in tranches, with rules agreed in advance:**
- **First tranche (now):** a modest share of the proposed increase. It goes only to agents, channels and regions whose recent customers are lost at a rate clearly below break-even.
- **Later tranches:** released on early signals, namely missed first payments and the share of scheduled payments collected by day 30. First check against past customers that these signals predict day-90 results, so you don't wait three months for each decision. Then confirm with the day-90 figures.
- **Board dashboard:**
  - customers still paying at day 90 and day 180;
  - payments collected as a share of payments due, by month of sale;
  - acquisition cost per *paying* customer;
  - share of locked customers who resume paying;
  - money tied up in devices held by customers who have stopped paying.

  New sign-ups stay on the dashboard but stop being the headline.

## 7. What would change my advice

- **Scale faster if** either of these is true:
  - most "deactivated" accounts start paying again, with healthy total payments over time; or
  - the day-90 loss stayed flat as volume grew, and customers from your recent expansions perform like your established base.

  In both cases, the model must also show that extra customers clearly pay back. Then the board is broadly right, and the tranches just become a quick confirmation.
- **Slow down further if** product or installation faults drive a large share of losses (fix them before selling more of those products), or if customers from recent expansions are already below break-even.

## 8. How to put it to the board

"We're not arguing against growth. We're arguing against paying to acquire customers who don't pay. After about six weeks of analysis and immediate fixes to onboarding, we'll know what the next 1,000 customers bought with extra spend are worth. We'll then put the money where they're profitable and scale there with confidence."
