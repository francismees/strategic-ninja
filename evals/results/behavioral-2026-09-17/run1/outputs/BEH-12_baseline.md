# Biscuits: the volume loss is a supply problem

## The insight

**People still want your biscuits. They just can't find them.** The 10% drop comes from the warehouse change, not from the brand, the price or the advertising. But if the shelves stay empty for long, it will turn into a real demand problem, because shops and shoppers will get used to buying a competitor instead.

## Why the numbers point there

**1. Missing stock alone explains most or all of the drop.**
In your top 500 outlets, the share of time you're on the shelf fell from 95% to 81%. Sales per stocked outlet haven't changed, so those outlets now sell 81/95 = 0.85 of what they did, a drop of about 15%.
- If these outlets are about two-thirds of your volume, that accounts for the whole 10% (0.68 × 15% ≈ 10%).
- If they're a smaller share (say 40%), it accounts for about 6 points. The rest is probably the same problem in smaller outlets served by the same warehouse, where you aren't measuring it.
- Each point of stock-outs you fix in the top 500 is worth roughly 0.5–0.7% of total volume.

**2. The sales are lost, not delayed.**
If shoppers went to another shop to find your brand, sales in outlets that still have stock would go up. They haven't. So when your biscuit is missing, people buy another brand or skip the purchase. That's normal for a cheap impulse snack bought in a duka or kiosk. It also shows that being on the shelf is the biggest driver of your volume.

**3. Shopkeepers are the bigger long-term risk.**
Duka owners have little cash and little shelf space. They stock whoever delivers reliably. Every week you're out, a competitor's rep can fill your space and build the ordering habit. Winning that space back later costs money, so this is urgent.

## Check these this week

These checks shouldn't hold up the fixes below.

1. **Find where stock gets stuck.** Is it missing from the warehouse, stuck between the warehouse and the outlet, or sitting in the shop's back room and not on the shelf? Each needs a different fix. Compare warehouse stock, order fill rate, delivery times and a sample of back rooms.
2. **Look for patterns.** Is the problem concentrated in certain products (usually the fast sellers), routes, areas or distributors? If the new site is further out, traffic can cut the number of deliveries each van makes per day.
3. **Look for common move problems.**
   - Reorder points or safety stock reset to defaults in a new system
   - Product codes not set up correctly, so orders don't go through
   - New minimum orders or credit holds for distributors
   - Humidity or rough handling causing broken or soft packs that shops reject
   - Short-dated stock picked first, so shops refuse it
4. **Check the trend.** Are stock-outs falling week by week (teething problems) or flat at 19% (a structural fault)?
5. **Check what "volume" means.** Is the 10% your sales to distributors or what shoppers actually buy? If distributor orders fell more than shopper sales, distributors are also running down their own stock.
6. **Check whether the measurement changed.** Are the stock-outs measured the same way (same auditors, same outlet list) before and after the move?

## What to do

### Now (weeks 0–2): stop the losses
- **Treat this as a supply emergency, not a marketing one.** Name one owner. Hold a daily meeting with sales, supply chain and the distributors, and track stock-outs in the top 500 every day.
- **Protect the most valuable outlets first.** Rank the 500 by volume. For roughly the top 100, guarantee delivery: dedicated drops, extra stock in the shop, and quick top-ups by boda boda where that makes sense.
- **Put the fast sellers first.** Make sure the products that bring in most of the volume are never out, even if slower lines have to wait for a while.
- **Add temporary capacity.** Use a nearby cross-dock or a distributor's depot, or keep part of the old warehouse running, until the new one proves it can deliver reliably.
- **Get stock from the back room onto the shelf.** Merchandisers can do shelf checks and send WhatsApp photos to confirm.
- **Don't let the forecast drop.** The planning system will read the missing sales as lower demand and cut supply, which makes stock-outs worse. Keep planning on demand from before the move until availability is fixed.

### Weeks 2–8: fix the cause
- Fix whatever the checks turn up, whether that's routes and delivery frequency, system settings, handling or distributor terms.
- Put service targets, with penalties, into the contract with the warehouse or transport operator. Suggested targets:
  - Top-500 stock-outs at 5% or below within about six weeks, then under 5%
  - On-time, in-full delivery to the top 500 at 95% or above
  - A fixed maximum time from order to delivery
- Keep the daily stock-out tracker as a permanent weekly dashboard with alert levels.

### Weeks 4–12: win back what was lost
Once outlets are restocked, sort them into three groups:
- **Sales back to normal:** no extra spend needed.
- **Restocked but sales still down:** a competitor has probably taken the shelf or the shopper. Spend here, with incentives for shopkeepers to restore your shelf space and display, plus visibility or sampling at the point of sale.
- **Still out of stock:** escalate to the owner.

Only this targeted win-back spending is justified. It should be local, not a national campaign.

## What not to do
- **Don't cut prices or run consumer promotions** to "recover volume." You'd be sending shoppers to empty shelves and giving away margin on biscuits you can't sell.
- **Don't change the brand or the advertising.** The data shows demand is fine. Consider pausing advertising in the worst-hit areas until stock is back.
- **Don't accept the lower volume as the new normal** in plans or targets.

## Bottom line
Getting top-500 stock-outs back to about 5% should recover most of the 10%, possibly all of it. It's the fastest and cheapest growth you have right now. The risk is time: the longer shelves stay empty, the more of this lost volume becomes permanent. Fix supply first and spend on marketing only where it's needed afterwards.
