# Campaign brief: [Brand] juice, Tanzania (conditional draft)

**Bottom line:** Here's the brief, set up so you can start now. One warning shapes it. "Our advertising got weak" is what the team believes, but nobody has checked it yet. A juice brand can lose 12% just as easily through shelf price, stock-outs, a distributor change, a competitor's price or cooler push, or a shrinking category, and a new campaign fixes none of those. So the parts of the brief that depend on the cause (the barrier, the communications task and the proposition) are written as scenarios. You lock them after a short check. I estimate the check takes 2–3 weeks, and most of it uses data you already have.

---

## 1. Before we brief creative: is advertising really the problem?

**The problem, stated without assuming the answer**
- **Decision:** whether to spend on a new campaign, and what that campaign must achieve.
- **Objective:** stop the decline and win back lost sales profitably.
- **What we've seen:** sales down 12% this year. This is the company's own figure, and we don't yet know whether it's value or volume, or sell-in or sell-out.
- **Suspected cause:** weaker advertising, one of several possible causes.
- **Proposed solution:** a new campaign.

**"Weak advertising" can mean four different things, and each needs a different fix:**
- **Less weight.** Spend, reach or weeks on air fell. The fix may be putting media weight back behind the existing ads, not making a new campaign.
- **Poor branding.** People remember the ads but don't connect them to the brand. The fix is in how the ads are made.
- **Wrong people or places.** For example, the ads mostly reached people who already buy the brand, or ran in regions where stock was poor.
- **Competitors got louder** while your activity stayed the same.

**Possible explanations to test against each other**

| Explanation | If true, we'd see | What would rule it out | What it means for the plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| Advertising weaker | Media weight or reach fell, or competitors' share of voice rose. Fewer category buyers think of the brand. The decline is broad, including outlets where stock and relative price held. | Media and brand measures are stable. Losses sit where stock or price changed. | Go ahead with the campaign brief (below) |
| Price / affordability | Shelf price rose against the main competitors, or a key pack moved to an awkward price point. Losses concentrate in those packs and outlets, and competitors gain there. | Losses are just as big where relative price didn't change | Fix price and pack first; advertising plays a supporting role |
| Availability / route to market | Fewer outlets stock the brand, stock-outs rise, less of it is chilled, or a distributor changed. Sales per stocking outlet stay stable. | Distribution is stable, but each outlet sells less | Fix distribution before spending on media |
| Competitor move | Share goes to one or two competitors in specific channels or regions, through a new entrant, price cut, cooler placements or new pack. | Share is stable, or losses are spread across all competitors | Copy whatever actually drove the competitor's gain (price, pack, coolers), not necessarily their advertising |
| Category shrinking or substitution | Competitors are down too and the brand's share holds. Volume moves to cheaper drinks or other substitutes. | Brand share falls while the category is flat or growing | Needs a category and pack-price strategy; brand advertising alone won't do it |
| Product or range | The decline sits in specific SKUs. A formula, pack or quality change happened, complaints rose or repeat buying fell. | The decline is spread evenly across the range | Fix the product or range; advertising can't make up for it |
| Measurement quirk | Distributor destocking, one lost key account, a gap between sell-in and sell-out, or periods of different length | Sell-out data show the same decline | Re-measure the size of the problem |

Several of these can be true at once; price and availability problems often come together. What matters is which one is holding sales back most.

**Checks, in order (cheapest and most revealing first)**
1. **Size and shape.** Is the 12% value or volume, sell-in or sell-out? Split the change into price, volume and mix by SKU and region. If value fell 12% while the average price per litre rose, volume fell by more than 12%.
2. **Where the loss sits.** Which regions (including Mainland vs Zanzibar), channels (dukas, kiosks, supermarkets, wholesale, forecourts) and packs account for most of it?
3. **Fewer outlets, or slower sales per outlet?** Compare the number of outlets stocking the brand and the stock-out rate with sales per stocking outlet, using the same splits.
4. **Price position.** Compare shelf prices with your two or three main competitors at key packs, by channel. For a first read, a store check of a few dozen outlets per priority region is enough.
5. **Category and competitors.** Are competitors down too? Use a retail audit, distributor intelligence or competitor volumes, whichever you have.
6. **Advertising itself.** Compare this year with last on media spend, reach and frequency, weeks on air and channel mix. Look at competitor activity, and at any brand tracking: how readily buyers think of the brand, and whether people who recognise the ads name the right brand.
7. **Talk to the trade.** Hold short conversations with distributors and retailers in the worst-hit regions about what changed. These point to causes; they can't tell you how big each one is.

**Decision rule.** If checks 3–5 explain most of the decline, hold creative production and spend first on distribution or price and pack. Advertising then plays a supporting role, such as announcing a new pack or wider availability. If the checks confirm advertising is the problem, finalise the brief below. If the picture is mixed, run the campaign only in areas where stock and price position are healthy.

## 2. The draft brief

### 1. Business problem and objective
[Brand] sales fell 12% this year [value/volume, period and scope to confirm]. **Objective:** recover [X] of the lost volume in [priority regions/channels] within [period], and make a positive return after campaign costs. Set X from the part of the loss the checks link to advertising, not from the headline 12%.

### 2. What we want people to do
Choose [Brand] more often when they buy juice. Buyer data decide which version matters:
- **If fewer people are buying:** win back lapsed and light buyers.
- **If existing buyers are buying less often:** connect the brand to more buying occasions.

Add goals about what people think or feel only if brand tracking shows those are driving the decline, for example if fewer buyers now think of the brand or rate it good value.

### 3. Who and when
Define the audience as people who buy juice in the priority regions, not only current loyal buyers. Describe them by the buying situation rather than by age or lifestyle labels. These occasions are guesses for now and need confirming with sales and outlet data:
- **Take-home buying for the household:** multi-serve packs from dukas and supermarkets. The buyer may not be the drinker, for example when buying for children.
- **Single-serve drinks, often chilled, drunk straight away:** bought at kiosks, forecourts and other on-the-go outlets.

Focus the brief on whichever occasion accounts for most of the lost volume.

### 4. Barrier *(not yet established)*
The checks could confirm one of three advertising problems:
- **A. Fewer buyers think of the brand when they're choosing.** To confirm: tracking shows fewer category buyers bring the brand to mind, media weight fell or competitors' share of voice rose, and distribution and price held steady.
- **B. People feel it's no longer worth the price.** To confirm: the price gap to competitors widened, losses concentrate where it widened, and retailers or shoppers say the brand isn't worth the extra. Advertising alone is unlikely to fix this; pair it with a price and pack change.
- **C. The ads ran, but people didn't link them to the brand.** To confirm: plenty of people recognise the ads, but few name the right brand.

### 5. Communications task
- **If A:** make the brand come to mind in the buying situations where volume was lost. That needs enough reach among juice buyers, and the brand's familiar colours, logo, pack and sounds used consistently.
- **If B:** give buyers a true, provable reason the juice is worth its price, launched together with the price and pack fix.
- **If C:** the current message and media weight may be fine. The job is ads where the brand can't be missed: shown early and often, using those familiar assets.

### 6. Single-minded proposition
*[Write this once the barrier is confirmed.]* It must be one thought that is true, provable and motivating in the focus occasion. Test it by imagining a competitor's logo on it: if it fits just as well, it isn't ours yet. Starting material is any product fact you can prove, such as fruit content, taste-test results, sugar content, sourcing, pack format or price per serving.

### 7. Reasons to believe
*[Take these from product specifications, test results and certifications. None have been supplied yet. Don't brief any claim you can't prove.]*

### 8. Brand assets
Review the brand's assets before changing anything: logo, colours, pack shape, any character, sound or music, and tagline. Keep and strengthen the ones buyers already connect with [Brand]. To find out which those are, show each asset without the brand name and ask people which brand it belongs to. A "fresh new look" that drops familiar assets can make the brand harder to recognise just when you need people to spot it.

### 9. Tone, language and culture *(needs local review)*
Kiswahili copy is the likely default for mass reach. Choose the style of Kiswahili (formal, conversational or youth/urban) for the specific audience. Then check it with native speakers from that audience in the target regions. Don't borrow Kenyan Sheng or Nairobi references. English or mixed-language copy for particular urban channels needs the same check. Use cultural references such as music, football, family or faith only where research with the target audience supports them.

### 10. Mandatories
- Legal/regulatory must check every product claim, especially "natural", fruit percentage, vitamins and sugar content. Each claim needs proof and must meet current Tanzanian food labelling and advertising rules.
- Take extra care with anything that could be read as aimed at children.
- Use current pack shots, follow brand guidelines, and use the languages agreed in section 9.

### 11. Channels and their roles
- **Reach:** pick media for the number of real juice buyers it reaches in priority regions. Followers, impressions and SIM or subscription counts don't tell you how many people you reached.
- **Point of purchase:** remind people at the moment they choose, with outlet branding and shelf and cooler visibility. Only do this in outlets with reliable stock.
- **Trade:** stock and brief distributors and retailers before launch, so the campaign doesn't send people looking for juice they can't find.
- **Digital/social:** include it only if there's evidence it reaches the focus audience, not as a default.

**Sequencing rule:** don't go on air in areas with known stock problems.

### 12. How success will be judged
**Test before scaling.** Launch in test regions and compare them with similar regions that don't get the campaign, or roll it out region by region. Measure where things stood before launch. That way the campaign's effect can be separated from changes in price, stock, season and competitor activity.

| Level | Measure | What it tells us |
|---|---|---|
| Reach | How many juice buyers in the test regions saw the campaign, and how often | That the campaign ran as planned. This alone is not success. |
| Brand | How readily buyers think of the brand in the focus buying situation; whether people who recognise the ads name the right brand | Whether the brand is more likely to come to mind |
| Buying | Sales per stocking outlet; number of buyers and repeat buying, if panel data exist | Whether buying changed |
| Business | Volume, value and share compared with the non-campaign regions; profit after campaign costs | Whether the campaign paid for itself |
| Guardrails | Number of outlets stocking the brand, stock-outs, shelf price against competitors | Whether something else explains the result |

**Starting point:** take it from the check data. If a measure doesn't exist yet (for example, there's no brand tracking), run a survey before launch.

**Rules to agree before launch:**
- **Scale** if the extra volume, compared with the non-campaign regions, clears the break-even level.
- **Check availability, price and the proposition before spending more** if brand measures improve but sales don't.
- **Stop or rethink** if neither moves after [a period covering several purchase cycles].

**Break-even:** extra volume needed = total campaign cost (production + media + point of sale) ÷ profit per litre (or case) after trade margins and variable costs. Compare that with the volume lost. If the campaign would have to win back more than the share of the loss caused by advertising, rethink the budget or the plan.

### 13. What we know and don't know

| Item | Status |
|---|---|
| Sales fell 12% this year | Company figure; value or volume, sell-in or sell-out, and scope unknown |
| Advertising weakened | Team belief; not yet checked |
| Media spend, reach and weeks on air vs last year | Unknown |
| Brand tracking (how readily buyers think of the brand, whether they link the ads to it) | Unknown |
| Distribution, stock-outs, price position against competitors | Unknown |
| Competitor and category trends | Unknown |
| Where the loss sits (region, channel, pack) | Unknown |
| Product facts to support a proposition | Unknown |
| Budget | Unknown |

### 14. Budget and timing basis
- **Budget:** set it from the break-even calculation and the share of the loss advertising can realistically win back, not from last year's spend.
- **Timing (my estimate):** checks take 2–3 weeks. Then lock sections 1–7, develop creative, launch in the test regions, read results after several purchase cycles, and decide whether to scale, change or stop.
- **Seasonality:** compare test and non-campaign regions over the same weeks, not against earlier months.

## 3. What would help me finalise this
Answers to these three questions would let me lock most of the brief:
1. Is the 12% value or volume, and sell-in or sell-out? Can you split it by region, channel or pack?
2. Did media spend, reach or weeks on air actually fall this year? Is there any brand tracking?
3. Did anything else change this year, such as your prices, distributors, pack or formula, or a competitor's price, pack or cooler activity?
