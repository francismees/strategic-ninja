# Young-adult beer persona, Dar es Salaam (working draft)

**The short version.** Below is a detailed persona you can use now to align the team and brief research. I changed two things in the brief, and both protect the work:

1. **Legal-age only.** On the widely used 1997–2012 definition, Gen Z is aged about 14 to 29 in 2026. About a quarter of its birth years (2009–2012) are still under 18.
2. **Defined by drinking behaviour, not birth year.**

One warning before you use it. The motivations, quotes and media habits are hypotheses I reasoned my way to, not research findings. I have no Dar consumer data for this brand, and **I wrote the quotes myself. No real person said them.** Use this to brief research and focus the team. Don't plan media or lock creative against it until the checks in section 5 are done.

---

## 1. Three fixes before this goes anywhere

**Say "legal-age young adults", not "Gen Z", in every document.** In Tanzania you have to be 18 to buy alcohol. Confirm the current position with legal, along with any broadcast and advertising rules on alcohol. For a beer brand, a brief that says "target Gen Z" reads as including 14–17-year-olds, and that's how a regulator, a journalist or a platform reviewer will read it. Use **"legal-age young adults, 18–29"** instead. Many brewers' own marketing codes go further, for example a minimum apparent age for talent (often 25) and adult-audience thresholds for media and creators. That's from general industry practice, not something I checked for this piece, so look at the codes that apply to you before you cast or buy media.

**A generation isn't a segment.** Birth year doesn't tell you who drinks beer, when, where or at what price. So the persona below stands for **legal-age Dar residents aged 18–29 who drink alcohol at least monthly and have beer in their repertoire.** That includes young adults who mostly choose spirits or other drinks today, who are your recruitment pool. It leaves out non-drinkers, who aren't a group to target.

**Make them a priority, not your only audience.** More on this in section 4.

## 2. The persona: Baraka, 25

*This is an illustrative composite. I picked the name, gender, job, neighbourhood and income to make him concrete; none of them comes from evidence. I don't know here whether young beer drinkers in Dar skew male, or by how much. Check that before you fix gender in a creative brief.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Age** | 25 |
| **Lives** | Rents a room in Sinza and splits costs with a cousin |
| **Works** | Sales at an electronics shop in Kariakoo. Base pay plus commission, so his income swings month to month |
| **Drinks** | Beer is his default with friends. When the group is big or money is short, they share a bottle of spirits |
| **Where** | Neighbourhood bars for football and after work, events and clubs now and then, home gatherings for birthdays |
| **Pays** | Cash or mobile money. Checks his balance before agreeing to a night out |
| **Brand behaviour** | Drinks two or three beer brands, depending on what's cold and what the group orders. Has a favourite but doesn't stick to it |
| **Not all his friends drink** | For some it's faith, family or personal choice. How many young adults in Dar drink at all is the first thing to measure |

**His week (hypothesis).** Weekdays mean work, long commutes and his phone. A big midweek match might mean two beers at the local bar with colleagues. Friday or Saturday is the main social night, and how much he spends depends on how commission went. Sunday is for recovery, family and more football. Money runs thin at month-end, and he's more generous with rounds after payday.

### What drives him

These are hypotheses. Each one says what it would mean for you and what evidence would disprove it.

1. **Belonging to the group, shown by buying rounds.** For him, drinking is mostly social. Buying a round and keeping up with others matters more than the brand.
   - *If true:* the group, or whoever is ordering, often picks the brand, so being the easy group default matters.
   - *Disproved if:* interviews show people pick their own brand no matter who's paying.
2. **Making the money last all night.** He judges drinks by what a night costs per person, not by the price of a bottle.
   - *If true:* on value, you compete with spirits shared across a group, not just with other beers.
   - *Disproved if:* young adults who switch to spirits say it's about taste or image, not cost.
3. **Enjoying himself without losing face.** Phones are out at every gathering, and being filmed drunk has a real social cost.
   - *If true:* pacing, lighter options and staying in control are part of the appeal.
   - *Disproved if:* moderation doesn't come up unprompted in interviews.
   - Several high-income markets widely report that younger adults drink less than earlier generations did at the same age. That's untested in Dar, not evidence about it.
4. **Keeping up to date.** He wants his drink, the places he goes and what he posts to look current. He doesn't want his father's drink.
   - *If true:* brand image, pack design and presence in young adults' spaces matter for whether he considers you at all.
   - *Disproved if:* young drinkers pick the same brands as older drinkers for the same reasons.
5. **Cold, now, here.** He drinks whatever is cold at the bar he's in. He won't walk to another bar for a particular brand.
   - *If true:* being chilled and in stock where young adults drink wins more share than advertising.
   - *Disproved if:* drinkers ask for a brand by name and wait, or go elsewhere to get it.

Drivers 1 and 4 describe why people might drink. **Your advertising can't claim them.** Many codes bar suggesting that alcohol brings popularity, social success or status.

### Barriers (hypotheses)

- **Cost of a night out against a budget that swings.** Official figures from the National Bureau of Statistics show pressure on household costs: in the year to August 2026, prices on the Mainland rose 4.3% overall and transport prices rose 13.8%. That describes prices across the whole country, not Baraka's budget. Whether the cost of getting home is shrinking young adults' going-out budgets in Dar is worth one research question.
- **Beer, or your brand, seen as a drink for older men.** This is untested, but it matters a lot: if it's true, the fix is brand image, not price.
- **Drinking around family.** He may drink discreetly where family or community disapprove. Handle this with care in research, and don't caricature it in creative.

### Example lines (written by me, not said by anyone)

Use these to brief moderators and creative teams on the hypotheses. Replace them with real quotes, in the audience's own mix of Kiswahili and English, as soon as you have them. No young adults from Dar have checked any of this.

- "If it's the derby, it's beer. Whoever buys this round, I get the next." *(driver 1)*
- "Before I say yes to going out, I check my balance." *(driver 2)*
- "There are five of us. A bottle of spirits goes further." *(driver 2)*
- "I don't want to be the one in someone's video at 2 a.m." *(driver 3)*
- "That's what my uncles drink." *(driver 4)*
- "Whatever's cold. I'm not waiting." *(driver 5)*

### Media habits (hypotheses to check, not a plan)

| Channel | Likely role in his week | Planning caution |
|---|---|---|
| **WhatsApp** (groups, Status) | Organising nights out, football banter, forwarding clips | Private. Brands get in through what people forward, not through ads |
| **Short-form video** (TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts) | Music, comedy and trends on commutes and in downtime | Platforms' alcohol ad policies differ by country, and some ban alcohol ads outright, so check each for Tanzania. Creators' audiences must be verifiably adult |
| **Instagram** | Finding venues and events, seeing what friends are doing | Age-gate brand accounts |
| **Music streaming and YouTube** | Local and regional music | Platform-reported listener numbers count accounts, not unique people |
| **Radio and TV** | Football on bar screens; radio in shops and on commutes | Check broadcast rules on alcohol advertising and timing |
| **Inside the bar** | Posters, coolers, bar staff, screens during matches | Probably closest to the moment he chooses. You can measure its effect through outlet sales |

Cost of mobile data may also shape when and how much video he watches, for example saving it for Wi-Fi. That's a hypothesis too.

**What the data we have can and can't say.** The Tanzania Communications Regulatory Authority (TCRA) counted 62.8 million internet subscriptions at the end of June 2026. That shows mobile internet is widespread across the country. But a subscription is a SIM card, not a person, and the figure isn't specific to Dar and says nothing about 18–29-year-olds or which platforms they use. Don't estimate reach from it. Use platform audience estimates (reported by the platforms themselves, not verified people), your agency's audience data, or a short media-use survey.

## 3. What would change your plan: four competing explanations

The persona is only worth having if it changes a decision. There are four different reasons young legal-age adults might not be choosing your beer today, and each one points your money somewhere else:

| If the main issue is... | Invest in... | Evidence that decides it |
|---|---|---|
| **They drink little or no alcohol** | Current beer buyers of all ages. Possibly a low- or no-alcohol option later, if the evidence supports it | How many drink, and how often: 18–29 compared with 30+ |
| **They drink, but spirits give more per person** | Price points and pack formats built for group rounds (check margin and whether you'd just take sales from your own packs) | Which drinks they buy, price paid per occasion, reasons for switching |
| **They drink beer, but you don't come to mind or don't feel like their brand** | Brand building around their drinking occasions, recognisable brand cues, presence in their spaces | Which brands come to mind for each occasion, by age, compared with competitors |
| **They'd choose you, but you're not cold where they drink** | Chilled stock and visibility in their outlets | Outlet checks: chilled availability and shelf price |

More than one can be true. The point is to find out which one is holding you back before you spend.

## 4. On "Gen Z are the future"

Part of this logic holds. The category needs a steady flow of new legal-age drinkers, and a brand that young adults don't drink will shrink over time. But three things need checking before this becomes your target:

- **Is the future pool as big as you assume?** If young adults in Dar drink less than older generations did at their age, "the future" is smaller than it looks. We don't know yet, so measure how many drink first.
- **Do early brand choices stick?** The payoff assumes a brand chosen at 20–25 is still chosen at 35. Check your own data. If your usage-and-attitude study or brand tracker asks drinkers which brands they used to drink, or you can follow the same people over time, see whether early choice predicts current choice. Answers that rely on memory only give a rough guide.
- **Where does today's volume come from?** In frequently bought categories like beer, brands mostly grow by winning more buyers across the whole category, not by concentrating on one group. That's a strong starting assumption to test against your own data. If most of your volume comes from buyers over 30, planning only for young adults pays for the future by starving the present.

**My recommendation:** make legal-age young adults your **priority investment audience**, meaning where you focus recruitment and learning. Your advertising and distribution should still reach all legal-age beer buyers.

## 5. Fastest way to turn this into evidence

In order of cost (timings are rough):

1. **Cut your existing data by age** (days). Take the brand tracker, usage-and-attitude study and sales by outlet type. For 18–24, 25–29 and 30+, look at: the share who buy you, how often, which brands they buy, and which brands come to mind for each occasion. Check the sample sizes, and check whether the tracker samples anyone under 25.
2. **Outlet checks** (one to two weeks). Visit 30–50 bars and liquor shops where young adults drink, across two or three areas of Dar. Record chilled availability, shelf price, what people are ordering and competitors' coolers. It won't be representative, but it's direct evidence on explanation 4.
3. **Qualitative research** (two to three weeks). Talk to pairs of friends or small groups of 18–29-year-old drinkers, ID-checked, including both beer and spirits drinkers and a range of incomes. Use native Kiswahili-speaking moderators, and record quotes noting whether each is translated. This replaces my example lines and confirms or rules out the five drivers.
4. **Short survey** (if steps 1–3 leave the big questions open). A few hundred legal-age adults in Dar, to measure how many drink, what they drink, when, what they pay and what media they use. Size segments and plan media against this, not against the persona.
