**My advice is not to name these five clusters or build personas on them for next year's targeting.** Your analyst's own notes show two problems. The clusters don't come out the same way twice, and they don't buy differently from each other. Personas would make five unstable groups look like real people, and those people would end up in a year of briefs, media plans and KPIs. Below I cover why, what to target on next year instead, and what a re-run has to pass before anything gets named.

One caveat: "no significant difference" doesn't prove the clusters buy the same way. The smaller clusters, especially cluster 5, are too small for a test to pick up a difference reliably. But without evidence that they buy differently, you have no reason to spend differently on them.

Personas also have a practical problem. The only description you have for each cluster is a one-word analyst label. Age, occupation, media habits, a day in the life and quotes would all have to be made up.

## What's probably going on

These are hypotheses to check, not findings. Which one is true changes the fix.

1. **There are no natural groups in these attitudes.** Attitudes may vary smoothly across people, and k-means will still slice them into five. The unstable runs and the failed split-half fit this. *Fix:* stop looking for attitude "types." Give everyone a score on each attitude and segment on behaviour.
2. **The clusters reflect how people use the rating scale.** Clusters built on agree/disagree statements can partly separate people who agree with everything from people who don't, rather than sorting them by what they believe. If the statements were asked in both Kiswahili and English, translation differences could add noise. *Check:* compare each cluster's average agreement across all 18 statements, then re-run after adjusting each respondent's answers for their own average level of agreement.
3. **Attitudes don't drive what people buy in this category.** Even real differences in attitude may not change purchases if habit, what the outlet stocks or the shelf price decide. The flat purchase results fit this. *Fix:* target on behaviour and use attitudes for messaging.
4. **The clusters are partly a city effect.** They may mostly reflect the mix of Dar es Salaam, Arusha and Mwanza respondents. *Check:* cross-tab cluster by city. If that's the pattern, target by city directly. That is simpler, and cities are easy to reach.

## What to target on next year instead

Don't let the plan wait for a re-run. The same survey already records how often people buy the category and the brand they bought last, so you can start from that.

1. **Pick the priority audience by behaviour.** Cut respondents by the brand they bought last (yours or a competitor's), how often they buy the category, and city. Choose the group that matches how you plan to grow:
   - competitor buyers and light buyers if growth has to come from more buyers;
   - your current buyers if it has to come from buying more often.

   Check the number of respondents in each group before committing.
2. **Treat the attitude themes as message routes, not audiences.** The themes are price, health, brand loyalty, novelty and status. First check that they hold up as consistent attitude scores (step 1 of the re-run below). Then test them as creative routes within the priority audience to see which one moves preference or purchase. That keeps the useful part of the study without staking your targeting on it.
3. **Keep broad reach among category buyers as the default.** No group has been shown to buy differently. A priority audience is where you concentrate effort; it doesn't mean shutting out other category buyers.

This recommendation depends on two things I can't see from the output: next year's growth objective, and whether each behaviour group has enough respondents to read.

## Before re-running the segmentation

Agree the pass rules **before** the analyst re-runs it, so nobody judges a solution after they've already grown attached to it.

1. **Run checks on the existing data:**
   - many random starts per run;
   - solutions from 2 to 6 clusters, compared;
   - a stability test for each cluster, not just the solution overall;
   - the scale-use and city checks above;
   - whether the 18 statements boil down to a few stable underlying attitudes.
2. **Segment on what changes the decision.** Where the survey captured them, use behaviour, occasion, outlet and pack first. Use attitudes to describe the segments, not to define them.
3. **Apply the pass rules.** A segment passes only if:
   - it shows up again across different seeds and in both halves of the sample;
   - it differs on at least one buying behaviour: frequency, brand bought, spend or outlet;
   - it has enough respondents after weighting to profile (55 is not enough);
   - you can reach it through a city, an outlet type or a media channel measured in the survey.
4. **Validate on fresh data.** Use a follow-up wave or a short repeat survey before committing media or trade budget.
5. **Set a decision point.** If a solution passes before your plan locks, add segment targeting to the plan. If not, run next year on the behaviour-based priority audience and revisit mid-year.

## If you need to refer to the clusters in the meantime

Call them "provisional attitude clusters 1–5 (not validated, not for targeting)" and keep the analyst's descriptive labels. I'd avoid catchy names. Once a group has a memorable name, it tends to show up in briefs and stay there long after the evidence stops supporting it.

## Persona template for a segment that passes

| Field | What goes in |
|---|---|
| Name | Describes behaviour and place, e.g. "frequent category buyers in Mwanza who last bought a competitor" |
| Size | Weighted share and estimated population, with a range |
| Commercial value | How often they buy the category, their spend, and your brand's share of their purchases |
| Behaviour | Brand bought last, outlet type, pack, occasion |
| Attitudes | Compared with the total sample; describe the segment, don't explain it |
| Where to reach them | City, outlet types and media, as measured in the survey, not assumed |
| What we do differently | Offer, pack and price point, message route, channel |
| How it makes money, and the KPI | More buyers, more frequent buying, or switching from competitors; name the metric and its starting level |
| Evidence strength | Stability result, number of respondents, whether it repeated on fresh data |
| Language check | Kiswahili copy and tone reviewed with people from the segment |
